# Cron expressions: next-run arithmetic that survives DST

## WHY

"Run this every night at 02:00" is the sentence a queue gets asked for more than any other, and it
is a sentence about a *wall clock* — which is where it stops being easy. The honest answer changes
when a government changes the rules, on a date nobody remembers, in a zone the process is not in.

Two ways to get it wrong, and both are invisible until they are expensive. The first is to keep a
table of next-run timestamps: forty schedules become forty rows somebody updates after every deploy,
and the one they forget is the report the customer reads at breakfast. The second is to wake up
every minute and ask "is it time yet?": cheap enough for one schedule, and a fixed cost per schedule
per minute for a fleet of workers that mostly have nothing to do.

The arithmetic that avoids both is small — one question, asked of an expression and an instant — and
it has exactly two days a year where a careless answer is wrong by an hour or by a whole run. Those
two days are this step. Everything else here is grammar: what a schedule is allowed to say, and what
it is refused, so that a typo is a message at submit time instead of a job that ran at the wrong
hour for a year.

## RECALL

- `time/clock-injection@apply` — `jobq/3.8-clock-injection`: the engine reads the clock through a
  port, which is what makes "when is the next run" a function from an instant to an instant rather
  than something a test has to wait for.
- `sched/fairness@design` — `jobq/3.9-priority-fairness`: which of the jobs that are *due* runs next,
  once several of them are.

## DELTA

Both of those decide about jobs that are already due. This step is about the instant itself: what an
expression means, and what the next instant it names is. Three things are deliberately **not** in it,
and each one is a decision somebody has to make out loud:

- **which runs you missed.** A schedule answers `next_after(t)`. "We were down for three days, what
  do we owe?" is a policy — every run, the most recent one, one and a count of what it swallowed —
  and a library that answered it would be answering for the process that has the context (the
  operator, the dashboard, the incident). A caller that wants a catch-up writes the loop; the loop
  is where the bound belongs.
- **seconds, years and names.** Five fields, numbers only, no `JAN`, no `MONDAY`, no `@daily`. A
  dialect that accepts more is a dialect where every caller guesses which subset the engine got.
- **the worker that sleeps.** Where these instants become a wake-up — and why that costs one entry
  per *queue* rather than one per job — is `jobq/3.11-delayed-timers`.

## TASK

- One type, `Schedule`, re-exported from the engine (`cron` is a `pub(crate)` module, like the rest
  of `src/engine`). Read an expression with `Schedule::parse`, and name the zone with
  `Schedule::in_zone` — an IANA name such as `Europe/Berlin`, refused rather than defaulted when it
  is not one the database has.
- `Schedule::next_after(after) -> Option<DateTime<Utc>>` is the whole API. The answer is an absolute
  instant, strictly after the one it was asked from, and `None` means "nothing, ever" — `0 0 31 2 *`
  is legal to write, so the search has a horizon and refuses to pretend it has an answer.
- The two day fields are a union: `0 0 1 * 1` runs on the 1st *or* on Mondays, and the rule keys on
  which of the two was written `*`. That is what a crontab already means, and the step's whole point
  is that a schedule written elsewhere keeps running the way its author expects.
- Name both DST rules in the module's documentation and in the spec's messages, because there is no
  default that is right for everybody: a reading the clock **skips** does not happen that day, and a
  reading the clock **repeats** happens twice — `next_after` from the first of the two answers the
  second.
- Refuse what the grammar does not have, and say which field: not five fields, a value outside the
  field's range, a range written backwards, a step of zero, an item that is not `*`, `N`, `N-M`,
  `*/S` or `N-M/S`.

## DONE WHEN

- `kiln run jobq/3.10-cron-schedules` — the twelve spec cases pass, including both DST days and the
  walk that asks from each answer in turn.
- `kiln check jobq/3.10-cron-schedules` — every must gate is green, including `clippy@3` (the new
  module is library code: no `unwrap`, no indexing, an `# Errors` section per fallible call) and
  `semver@baseline` (the surface gained names: `Schedule`, `parse`, `in_zone`, `next_after`).
- `cargo test -p jobq --test spec_cron` runs offline and takes milliseconds: no clock, no store, no
  container, because a schedule is arithmetic.

## TRAP

1. **ANDing the two day fields.** `0 0 1 * 1` then runs once a month instead of every Monday, and the
   quiet version of this bug is a schedule a customer wrote for another cron, moved to this engine,
   and now runs twelve times a year. The spec asserts the union from both sides for that reason.
2. **Doing the arithmetic in UTC and reading the zone somewhere else.** `0 9 * * *` fires at 09:00
   UTC in every zone: the code looks right, the test that uses one zone passes, and the two-zone
   case is the one that fails.
3. **Keeping the offset instead of the zone.** A fixed `-05:00` is correct from November to March and
   an hour wrong from March to November — which is precisely the window in which nobody looks.
4. **Running a skipped reading at "the same time, plus the gap".** On the day New York's clock jumps
   from 02:00 to 03:00, `30 2 * * *` becomes a run at 03:30 local: a time the expression never named,
   invented by the code that was supposed to be avoiding surprises.
5. **Handling only one half of the repeated hour.** Returning the first 01:30 and then the next day's
   drops a run once a year with no line in any log; returning the *same* instant when asked from an
   answer — the classic off-by-one in the comparison — makes a worker that fires on one instant for
   ever.
6. **Searching without a bound.** `0 0 31 2 *` is a legal expression and there is no instant it
   names: a loop that keeps looking for one is a worker that never comes back, and the bug shows up
   as a hung process with a config file nobody suspects.
7. **Defaulting an unknown zone to UTC.** `Europe/Berlim` runs the job five hours early for a year,
   and the only symptom is a report that arrives before its input does.
8. **Accepting `*/0`.** A step of zero names nothing, and every question about the schedule then runs
   to the horizon and answers `None`: a schedule that is silently empty is worse than one that does
   not parse.

## FURTHER

- `crontab(5)` — the man page the union rule and the `7`-is-Sunday spelling come from; read it for
  the parts this step refuses on purpose.
- `chrono`'s `LocalResult` and `TimeZone::from_local_datetime` — the three answers a local time can
  have, including the two the DST cases are made of.
- `chrono_tz::Tz` and the IANA time zone database behind it — a zone is a name plus a history of
  offsets, which is why this step takes a name and not a number.
- RFC 5545 (`RRULE`) — the same problem with a much larger grammar and the same two DST days; the
  places it disagrees with cron are worth knowing before anyone stores a schedule as a rule.
- `jobq/3.11-delayed-timers` — where these instants become a structure a worker sleeps on, and why
  the cost of waiting is the number of queues rather than the number of jobs.
