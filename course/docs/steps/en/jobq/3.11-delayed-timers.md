# Delayed jobs: a timer that costs nothing while it waits

## WHY

Every turn this worker has taken so far ended the same way: it found nothing to do, slept for the poll
interval, and asked again. That is a fine default and a bad habit — the queue is asked once per
queue per turn for ever, whether or not anything is scheduled, and the cost is the same for a queue
with one job due in a second and for a queue with a million jobs due next year.

The failure mode that makes it a *design* problem rather than a tuning problem is the shape of the
work: a scheduler whose jobs are mostly in the future — a report at 02:00, a retry in an hour, a
weekly export — spends almost all of its turns asking about work that cannot be done yet. Add
replicas and it multiplies: ten workers in a poll loop are ten queries per interval per queue, and the
database's answer is "not yet" every time.

The other half is what makes this subtle. The obvious fix — sleep until the next job's `run_at` —
is only as good as the answer it sleeps on, and that answer is a snapshot taken by one process about a
table every other process writes to. This step is about holding the deadline and keeping the poll
interval as the floor under it, so the wake-up is early and cheap when it can be, and never later than
the loop it replaces.

## RECALL

- `time/clock-injection@apply` — `jobq/3.8-clock-injection`: every instant in this engine comes from a
  clock it was handed, which is what makes "this job is due in two hours" a case that runs in a few
  hundred milliseconds instead of a two-hour test.
- `db/atomic-claim@design` — `jobq/3.7-leases-and-recovery`: the claim, the lease and the reclaim that
  put a row back in its queue. Those are the events that move a deadline, and they are why the
  structure here is a cache of the store's answer rather than a source of truth.

## DELTA

`sched/fairness@design` (`jobq/3.9-priority-fairness`) answers *who* runs when several jobs are due,
and `sched/cron@apply` (`jobq/3.10-cron-schedules`) answers *when* a scheduled instant is. Neither one
touches the question this step is about: what a worker does with the time in between, and what it
costs.

What is deliberately **not** here: the sleep call is not the lesson, and neither is a configuration
knob. The lesson is the *set* — one entry per queue with work, holding the instant that queue next
becomes claimable — because that is what turns "a million jobs scheduled a year out" from a million
wake-ups into sixty-four entries and one query per queue per turn. A structure that held the jobs
would have to be fed by reading them, which is the poll loop with more memory.

## TASK

- Add `ReadySet` to the engine (`timers` is a `pub(crate)` module, re-exported like the rest):
  `insert(queue, at)` records that a queue's next work is due at an instant and *replaces* whatever it
  owed before, `remove(queue)` forgets a queue that owes nothing, `next_deadline()` answers the
  earliest of them, and `len()` counts queues.
- Ask the store the one question the ready set needs an answer to: `Store::next_due(queue)` returns
  the earliest `run_at` among that queue's **queued** rows, or `None`. It goes on the port rather than
  in one backend because the worker holds a `dyn Store`, and both backends have to answer it — the
  memory scan and `min(run_at)` over the partial index the claim already uses.
- Wire the worker: a turn that finds nothing asks each queue when it next has work, and then waits
  until the earliest of those instants — `ReadySet::wait(now, floor)`. Keep
  `WorkerSettings::poll_interval` as the **floor**: a deadline may make the worker look sooner, never
  later, so a queue nothing has told this worker about is still polled and a stale deadline costs a
  turn rather than a stall.
- Give the worker the clock it measures against (`Worker::with_clock`, defaulting to `SystemClock`),
  because the wait is a subtraction between two wall-clock instants and a worker with a clock of its
  own would be the second authority `jobq/3.8-clock-injection` exists to remove.

## DONE WHEN

- `kiln run jobq/3.11-delayed-timers` — the six spec cases pass: the structure's own behaviour, a
  hundred thousand deadlines over sixty-four queues, the wait table, and two end-to-end cases.
- `kiln check jobq/3.11-delayed-timers` — every must gate is green, including `slow:conformance@2`
  (the spec runs on both backends, because `next_due` is a port method) and `semver@baseline` (the
  surface gained names).
- The end-to-end case is the one to read: a worker whose poll interval is ten seconds settles a job
  due in 200ms, well inside a deadline a polling worker could not meet.

## TRAP

1. **One entry per *job*.** A `Vec<Job>` of everything scheduled is the poll loop with a bigger
   allocation: it has to be refreshed by reading the rows, it changes size with the queue's depth,
   and the promise of this step — the cost is queues, not jobs — is gone.
2. **Recording deadlines instead of applying them.** A queue's next due instant is replaced when it is
   claimed, submitted, reclaimed or retried; a structure that pushed each new answer and kept the old
   ones reports an instant that is no longer anybody's, so a worker wakes for work nobody will hand
   it — and the memory grows with every claim.
3. **Forgetting the queue that owes nothing.** `None` from the store has to *remove* the entry, not
   leave the last instant behind: a worker that keeps a stale deadline sleeps through the job
   submitted a moment later.
4. **Sleeping exactly the deadline.** The deadline is a snapshot of a table other processes write to,
   and the answer can be wrong in both directions; with no floor under it, one stale answer becomes a
   queue that is not asked again for an hour. The floor is the difference between an optimisation and
   a new failure mode.
5. **Waiting zero for a deadline that has already arrived.** Work that is due and was not claimed is
   being held by something other than the clock — a per-queue cap, another worker that took the row
   in between — and polling immediately for it is an idle worker spinning against the store.
6. **Reading the store once and trusting it for ever.** The answers are cached in a structure the
   worker owns, and the cache is only as fresh as the last turn: anything that moves a deadline
   outside this process is invisible until the floor expires, which is exactly why the floor is not
   negotiable.
7. **Measuring the wait with the wall clock and the sleep with a monotonic one.** The deadline is a
   wall-clock instant and the wait is an interval: converting between them needs the injected clock,
   and a worker that reads `SystemTime::now` to do it is the second authority again — `clippy@3`
   refuses the call, which is the cheap half of this trap.
