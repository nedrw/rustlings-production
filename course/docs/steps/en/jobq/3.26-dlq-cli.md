# The dead-letter queue from the command line: look, replay, and drop with a reason

## RECALL

- `ops/dlq-tooling@apply` — `jobq/3.16-dlq-tooling`: **the port this door is written against.** That
  step named the queue (`dead_letters()`: a view over the rows in `failed`, not a collection of its
  own) and decided what one more life writes (`revived(job, now)` — the count kept, the ceiling
  moved to `attempts + 1`, `last_error` kept, the lease cleared, `run_at` from the store's clock),
  and it put the move on the port as `Store::replay(&JobId)`, one transaction for the row and the
  trail line. Its own summary says what it left out: *the acts an operator's tool takes*. This step
  is that half, and it does not change a word of `revived`.
- `cli/contract@apply` — `jobq/3.15-cli-contract`: **the grammar this step grows, and the four rules
  it keeps.** `jobqc` answers on stdout and diagnoses on stderr, one exit code per failure class,
  `--json` is a format rather than a second command, and a listing answers what the handle may see.
  `submit`, `inspect`, `list` and `usage` are already there; the dead-letter group joins the same
  parser and the same table of codes rather than starting a second tool beside it.
- `db/port-conformance@apply` — `jobq/3.5-store-port-conformance`: **why the acts are port methods
  and not queries a command assembles.** The port is the seam that makes "the same question, two
  implementations" a promise with a witness, and the conformance gate re-runs this step's spec
  once per backend. An act that only the in-memory store answered correctly would be exactly the
  defect that gate exists to find.
- `db/transactions@design` — `jobq/3.6-transaction-isolation`: **the trail, and the promise the
  record borrows from it.** A settle changes the row and appends the line that describes it in one
  transaction. The record of an *act* has to be at least that strong, and the reason is sharpest
  for the discard: a delete that committed without its line is work that disappeared with nobody
  recorded as having thrown it away.
- `sec/authz-tenant-isolation@operate` — `jobq/3.13-queue-authz-attribution`: **the handle is the
  identity, and a name is a claim.** A scoped store answers for the queues its model names, and a
  row it may not see is answered like a row nobody wrote — one answer for two cases, so a caller
  cannot tell them apart. The `--by` this step demands is not a credential (`jobq/3.13` is where
  "may I" is answered); it is what the record keeps.

## DELTA

**Everything an operator needs to *decide* already exists; what is missing is the door.** One step
back the port could name the dead letters and put one back, and one step before that the command
line could submit, inspect and list. Neither of them is something a person points at a queue at two
in the morning: the first is a library, and the second has no word for a dead letter in it. This
step is the missing half of both, and its decisions are about the **acts** — which is why they live
in shapes rather than in a help text.

Three decisions, and each one is a failure somebody meets:

- **an act is signed.** An `Order` carries the name of whoever takes it, and `Order::check` refuses
  an act with no name **before the store reads anything**. The trail of `jobq/3.6` records *what*
  happened to a job — a `replayed` line and the attempt it named — and no amount of reading it
  afterwards answers "who put this job back", which is the first question anybody asks about an act
  somebody else can watch. The name is a *claim* rather than a credential (this store trusts the
  handle it is handed, `jobq/3.13`), and a record of claims is still the difference between "an
  operator replayed this" and nobody knowing there was an operator at all.
- **the act that cannot be undone has to say why.** A replay is reversible: the job goes back into
  its queue with its payload and its counters, and a wrong one is a job that gets another attempt.
  A discard is not — the work is thrown away, and nothing left in the job can say what it was for.
  `Order::check` therefore refuses a discard whose reason is blank (empty, or nothing but
  whitespace) *before the row is read*, and `discardable(job)` refuses a job that is not a dead
  letter: a `queued` or `running` job is owed an attempt that is about to happen, and a `succeeded`
  one is work somebody already got, so throwing either away is a decision about something that is
  not an abandoned failure. The refusal is a *check* rather than a warning because a record with
  one unexplainable line in it is a record nobody trusts.
- **the record outlives the row it is about.** The trail table of `jobq/3.6` ends with the job it
  belongs to (`on delete cascade`), and its comment says in so many words that whether a purge
  keeps the trail of what it removed is the decision of the step that writes one. This is that
  step, and the answer is a record of its own with **no foreign key**: after a discard there is no
  row left to ask, and that is exactly when the line has to survive. `JobAudit` is that line — job,
  queue, action, actor, instant, reason — and `Store::operate` writes it in the same transaction as
  the change it describes.

The door has four decisions of its own, and the interesting one is what a refusal leaves behind:

- **`dlq list` is the operator's view, not a listing.** `jobqc list --state failed` asks the same
  question and answers with the whole job, payload included. What an operator decides with is how
  many attempts each row has spent and the sentence its last attempt died with; the payload is what
  they must *not* have — in a scrollback, in a ticket, in a pastebin. A tool that prints a
  customer's data in the middle of an incident has turned one incident into two, which is why the
  four-field line is its own shape rather than a flag on `list`.
- **a refusal is a class.** `2` when the call was wrong (an unknown subcommand, a missing flag, a
  blank reason, a job that is not a dead letter), `3` when the identity is not there, `4` when the
  store refused, `1` when the deployment is broken. Those numbers are the table `jobq/3.3` wrote and
  `jobq/3.15` made a contract; nothing here invents a fifth.
- **there is no batch.** `dlq replay <ID> <ID> <ID>` is a loop in the shell. A command that acted on
  two identities and refused a third has no single class to leave behind, and printing the two that
  worked beside a failure would hand a script the half-document `jobq/3.15` forbids. One identity,
  one act, one answer, one code — and `dlq audit` is where an operator reads what a scripted loop
  did.
- **the answer of an act is the line the record was given**, in the same shape `dlq audit` prints,
  because that is what just happened. A command that answered with something it had not written
  down would be a command whose answer and whose record can disagree.

Two names are added to the port, `Store::operate(&Order) -> JobAudit` and
`Store::acts(limit) -> Vec<JobAudit>`, with three promises: the row is read under a lock before it is
decided on (two operators cannot both be told they moved it), the change and the line are one
transaction, and a refused act writes nothing. A replay taken through this door leaves **two**
lines, and that is not duplication: the trail line is about the job and survives with it, the
record's line is about the act and the person, and it is still there when the job is long gone.

## TASK

The scaffold added a module, a page skeleton and a harness. **Nothing declares the new module yet**:
wiring `src/engine/mod.rs` is your first decision, and until it is made neither the spec nor the
harness compiles — which is the failure `kiln run jobq/3.26-dlq-cli` shows you first.

### `Act`, `Order`, `JobAudit` — the shapes the step is written in

`Act` is `Replay`/`Discard`, `#[non_exhaustive]` like every list in this crate's public vocabulary
(`jobq/3.2`), and `as_str()` is the one spelling the migration's check constraint, the durable
backend's decoder and somebody's `grep` all agree on. `Order` is a value rather than four arguments,
because the three things that can be wrong with an act — the name, the reason, the job — are then
checked in one place instead of at every call site, and because it carries the job's **identity**
rather than a copy of the row: the row is the store's to read, under a lock, and an order that
carried a copy would let a caller decide about a job that has moved since. `JobAudit::of(order, job,
at)` settles the two things the *record* says rather than the backend: the actor is trimmed (two
spellings of one name in a record is a record nobody can count) and a reason that is only whitespace
becomes no reason at all. `stored(…)` is the same line built from columns, for the backend that
reads them back.

### `Order::check` and `discardable` — the two refusals

`check` answers the first thing wrong about the *act* (no name; a discard with no reason) and
`discardable` the thing wrong about the *row* (not `failed`). Splitting them is the shape of the
promise a store makes: an act that is wrong in itself is refused before the read, an act that is
wrong for this row before the write, and a refused act leaves no line. `discardable` names the state
the job is in, because "already owed an attempt" and "finished" are two different answers about a
row an operator is looking at.

### `Store::operate` / `Store::acts` — the port, and both backends

Add the two methods to `Store` and implement them twice:

- the in-memory store appends lines to a vector under the same lock that moves the row, and keeps
  one private replay path (`replay_of`) and one private discard path (`discard_of`) so that
  `jobq/3.16`'s `Store::replay` keeps its signature: the same arithmetic behind two doors, one of
  which has nobody's name on it and writes no line;
- the durable store reads the row `for update` inside the transaction, puts the predicates in the
  statements (`state = 'failed'` for the delete, so a row that moved since the lock is not removed),
  and inserts the line in the same transaction; the delete cascades to the trail, so the record's
  line is what is left.

A store that took an act and wrote no line is a case this crate answers rather than panics on
(`unwritten`): it is unreachable while both backends write a line exactly when the order has one,
and the day it stops being unreachable it is the failure this whole step is about.

### `migrations/0008_job_audit.sql` — the table, forward-numbered

Four decisions belong in the file rather than in a sentence somewhere: **no foreign key** to `jobs`
(the row may be gone, and it is *because* it may be gone that the line has to survive); **the unit is
an act, not a job** (the same job replayed once and thrown away later is two lines, a refused act is
none, so there is no unique index to defer to); **the queue is denormalised** (an auditor reading the
line must not have to look the job up — and the queue is what the read is scoped by, in the shape
`jobq/3.13` established); and **`seq` is what orders it** (two acts can share an instant, and the
record still has an order: when the lines were appended rather than what the clock read).

### `src/cli/dlq.rs` — the group

`dlq list|replay|discard|audit` on the parser of `jobqc`, with `--queue`, `--limit`, `--by`,
`--reason` and the globals accepted on either side of the subcommand. Every mistake a caller can make
is refused here, before any setting is read and before any store is opened, so that `2` means "the
call was wrong" whether or not a database turns out to be reachable. `parse` builds an `Order` (the
act as a value the engine can check) rather than remembering the rule "a discard has a reason and a
replay does not" in a flag table, and `execute` prints the line the store wrote. The binary's help
text is the library's `USAGE`, so the grammar has one copy.

### `docs/dlq.md` — the page

The scaffold left a skeleton with `TODO` markers; fill it in for the person who has just been woken
up. What is stuck and how to read a line of it, the two acts and what each one costs, the record,
the table of exit codes a script branches on, and the first five minutes of an incident in order —
including the step that is easy to skip: *fix the cause first*, because replaying a thousand jobs
into a broken handler is a thousand dead letters again in ten minutes. The spec reads this page, so
it is a deliverable rather than a comment on one.

### `tests/dlq_harness.rs` — the course's instrument

It drives the in-memory store through `Order`/`operate`/`acts` and prints four counts —
`jobq-budget acts=…`, `record_lines=…`, `orphans_max=…`, `unattributed_max=…` — which
`slow:bench-budget@10` compares against `course/tracks/jobq/budget/3.26-dlq-cli.toml`. Counts, never
seconds: the gate answers the same thing on a loaded machine as on an idle one. The harness is the
course's file, not yours — editing it edits the measurement, and `kiln reset jobq/3.26-dlq-cli` puts
it back.

## TRAP

1. **A command that decides from the row it was handed.** Reading the dead letter with
   `dead_letters()` and then writing a second `update` of its own is the shorter version of this
   step and the bug it exists to prevent: between the read and the write the row can move (another
   operator, the worker, a purge), and two operators are then both told they moved it. The act goes
   through `Store::operate`, which reads the row under its own lock and decides in the same
   transaction — and the actor's name is on the order rather than in a comment above the command.
2. **A record written before the row is checked.** Inserting the line and *then* discovering that
   the job is not a dead letter (or that it is gone, or somebody else's) leaves a line about an act
   that did not happen — the one thing an auditor cannot use. The order is: the order's own
   refusals, then the read, then the write, and both backends refuse before they touch anything. The
   spec's `an_act_that_does_not_hold_changes_nothing` asserts the record is empty afterwards, so a
   `record` call that runs before the check turns five cases red at once.
3. **A foreign key on the record.** `references jobs (id) on delete cascade` looks like keeping the
   data honest and is the opposite: the discard is the delete, so the cascade removes the line
   exactly when it became the only account of the work. The durable backend also removes the trail
   by *predicate* rather than by cascade (`delete from jobs where id = $1 and state = 'failed'`),
   for the reason 3.23 gives about predicates: a second statement can be reasoned about, a trigger
   cannot.
4. **`--reason ""` accepted, or a reason trimmed after the decision.** `is_empty()` lets a reason of
   one space through and `trim()` after the check leaves the untrimmed text in the record; a record
   with two spellings of one name, or a discard with a reason that is only whitespace, is a record
   whose count of "why" is the count of keystrokes. The check is `trim().is_empty()` on both the
   name and the reason, and `JobAudit::of` trims what it stores.
5. **Replacing `Store::replay` with the new door.** It is tempting to route `jobq/3.16`'s method
   through `operate` — and then the replay has to have a name, which it does not: `run_at`, `attempts`
   and the trail line are the same, and the record line is not. The two doors share `replay_of` and
   differ by one option, so the arithmetic exists once and the *record* decides nothing about the
   row.
6. **A batch, because the shell loop is slow.** `dlq replay a b c` reads well and has no answer for
   "two worked and one was refused": the exit code cannot be one class, and stdout cannot be half a
   document. The spec's first case asserts that two identities are a usage error naming the second.
7. **The payload in `dlq list`.** The obvious implementation is `jobqc list --state failed` with a
   flag, and it puts customer data into a terminal, a ticket and a pastebin. `dlq list` is four
   fields, none of them the payload, and the JSON shape has no `payload` key at all — the spec asks
   for the key and expects its absence.
8. **A second copy of the grammar in the binary.** Handing `jobqc`'s help text somewhere else and
   printing it from `main` makes the two copies drift the first time a flag is added: the spec
   compares the binary's `--help` with `cli::USAGE` byte for byte.
9. **Editing the harness or the baseline instead of the code.** `tests/dlq_harness.rs` is the
   course's file and `course/tracks/jobq/budget/3.26-dlq-cli.toml` is the copy the gate reads:
   editing either changes the instrument, not the thing it measures.
10. **A page with `TODO` still in it.** The spec reads `docs/dlq.md` and refuses a placeholder, so an
    otherwise perfect step stays red until the page is written — which is the point: the operator
    woken at three in the morning does not read the source.
