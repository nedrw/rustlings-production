# Error budgets and alerts that deserve to wake someone

## RECALL

- `obs/metrics-slo@apply` — `relay/2.18-delivery-metrics`: **the numbers a delivery service exports**, and
the three rules that shape them — a histogram rather than an average, a gauge read from the queue at
the moment it is scraped rather than remembered, and a label set the service *chose* because every
label value is a series in every collector. This queue has the same three problems and none of that
implementation: what it has are the counters of `jobq/3.13-queue-authz-attribution`, and what it
does not have is a decision about what they are *called* or what a person is supposed to do when one
of them moves. That decision is this step.
- `obs/structured-logs@apply` — `mailer/1.6-logging`: **one line is data, not a sentence.** A
collector reads fields out of it, the run the line belongs to is a *span* rather than an argument
everybody remembers to pass, and the one thing that never appears is a secret. That step's lines
were written on a request path, where the span is entered by the framework. Here there is no
framework: the lines are written by a worker loop and by the tasks it spawns, and a span does not
follow a task.
- `delivery/dlq@operate` — `jobq/3.16-dlq-tooling`: **the view an operator uses after an alert.** A
rule that says "this queue is losing work" is only useful if the next question — *which job, and
why* — has an answer, and that answer is the dead-letter view and the attempt's own words. This step
is where the two halves meet: the sample says how much, the line says which, and the drill is what
makes both of them reproducible.

## DELTA

**A count is not a signal, and this step is the distance between them.** `jobq/3.13` counts five
things per queue; a counter nobody names is a number in a table, and a name nobody agreed on is a
dashboard that disagrees with the person reading it. Three things are missing, and each of them is a
sentence a runbook can hold:

- **the table.** Which families exist, what shape each one has, and what it is called. A *counter*
  is cumulative, so the question asked of it is "how fast" — and the answer is the difference between
  two readings, which is what a rule's window is. A *gauge* is a reading, and asking how fast a gauge
  is rising is a question about the scrape rather than about the queue. Both halves of that are the
  naming decision, and the name is what a rule writes;
- **the sample.** The numbers live in the store (`jobq/3.13`), the lines live in the process, and the
  thing that makes them one system is that the line's fields *are* the family names. That is not
  cosmetic: a collector derives the series from the line, so a field the process forgot to write is a
  series that does not exist, and a field the page has a rule for is a rule that never fires;
- **the rule.** A threshold on its own is not a rule. It needs the signal, the window it is measured
  over, and the drill that triggers it on purpose — and the drill is the part that makes the other
  three more than a paragraph.

**What a budget of failures means for *this* queue** is the part `relay/2.18` could not answer, and
it is three decisions:

1. **the unit is the attempt that settled** — not the job that arrived, and not the attempt that
   started. A queue whose producers stopped has a failure rate of zero and no work; the budget is
   about settles, what is *arriving* is a different signal, and what is *stuck* is the gauge;
2. **a retry is the budget working and a reclaim is a loss.** An attempt that fails and is handed
   back costs the job an attempt and the queue nothing — that is the engine doing what it was built
   for. An attempt that ends `reclaimed` is work nobody finished: a worker died holding it, or its
   lease ran out. No failure counter in this crate moves for either one of those, and a budget that
   reads only `failed` reads **zero** through a deployment that is losing every job it claims. This
   is the single most important thing in the step;
3. **a share below the floor is not a claim about a queue.** One failure in one attempt is a 100%
   failure rate, and it is also a Tuesday. A rule needs a denominator before it is allowed to page,
   and the floor is a number in the page rather than a habit of the person reading it.

The window is a fourth decision that falls out of the first: a counter is cumulative from the day the
queue was created, so "the failure rate" is not a property of a reading. It is the **difference
between two**, which is why the arithmetic takes two readings and why every rule has a window column.

**The two grains, and why both.** The sample answers *how much*: one line per queue, one field per
family, at zero before anything has happened — a series that appears only after the first failure
answers "no data" during exactly the quiet period somebody is checking. The attempt line answers
*which* and *why*: queue, job, attempt, worker, outcome, the handler's own words and how long it took.
Neither replaces the other, and the path from one to the other is the whole point of a queue's
observability: a rule fires on the share, and an operator finds the jobs and their reasons in the
lines.

**The run is context, and a task is a new context.** `mailer/1.6` made the run a span with `run.id`
so that no call site has to remember it. That is a stronger promise than it looks, because this
process writes lines from three places: the daemon that starts it, the worker loop, and one task per
job. The daemon's own lines inherit the span; the tasks are *started* by it and inherit nothing —
`tokio::spawn` gives a task an empty context, deliberately, because a task outlives the call that
made it. So the span has to be put on what you spawn, and the failure mode without it is not an
error: it is a deployment whose attempt lines cannot be attributed to the run that wrote them, which
is the line an operator reads after an incident.

## TASK

The scaffold added two files, and **nothing declares the first one**:

- `src/service/signals.rs` — four bodies and the argument for each, with the types in place. Wiring
  `pub mod signals;` into `src/service/mod.rs` is your first decision, and until it is made the spec
  does not compile, which is the step telling you what it wants;
- `docs/alerts.md` — the page, as a stub. It has the sections and the table's columns with their
  targets left as placeholders: the `links` gate is red until every target resolves, and the spec is
  red until every rule names something real.

### `src/service/signals.rs`

1. **`signals()`** — the families. A name a rule can write, a kind, the labels every series carries,
   and one sentence of help. The label set is the axis the numbers are cut by, and it is the same
   decision `relay/2.18` made about a delivery: the queue's *name* is bounded and configured, and a
   job id, a worker or a payload would be a series per job;
2. **`Sample::emit()`** — the line a run reports: the label, then one field per family. The field
   list is literal because that is what a subscriber indexes, so the test that keeps the two
   spellings of the names equal is the point rather than a formality;
3. **`Attempt::emit()`** — the line about one attempt. The fields are the same on every outcome (an
   empty reason rather than a missing field, because a field that is only sometimes there is a query
   that has to handle it), the *level* is what differs, `took_ms` is a number rather than a formatted
   duration, and the payload is not a field at all;
4. **`Budget::burn_between()`** — two readings in, a window out: what each counter gained, which of
   those gains count against the budget, and what the share is measured against.

### The wiring

Four places, and each one is a decision rather than a line:

- **`logging::init`** writes the install line inside the run's span, and the span has to be built
  *after* the subscriber is installed — a span claimed from no dispatcher is a span nobody records,
  and the first line of every run would be the one line missing from it;
- **`service::serve`** is the run: the two tasks it starts are handed the span, because a task does
  not inherit one. Its last act is the sample: one line per queue the deployment drains, read from
  the store, which is where the counters live — the process is reporting a reading, not its own
  lifetime;
- **`worker::run`** hands the span to the task per job, for the same reason, one layer down;
- **`worker::handle_one`** writes both endings through the one shape, so the fields cannot be
  different on the two paths.

### `docs/alerts.md`

A rule table, one row per family at least, each row naming its signal, its threshold, its window, the
drill that triggers it and what to do. It also states the budget the drill measures against: the doc
uses `Budget::standard()`, and the spec compares the page's numbers with the code's — a page that
says one thing while the drill measures another is a page that has stopped being true, and nothing
else in the tree would notice.

### What deliberately does not change

Every promise `jobq/3.1`–`jobq/3.16` certified: the `--check-config` keys, the `draining` line, the
signal's exit code, the CLI's grammar and exit codes, the port's signatures, and the trail. This step
adds fields to lines the daemon already writes and one module it did not have; it removes one field,
and it says why: the payload is the row's, and a line is data a collector indexes.

  the trail, it does not change them.

## TRAP

1. **A budget that counts only failures.** The intuitive implementation is `failed / claimed`, and
   it reads as healthy through the worst outage this queue has: a deployment whose workers die
   holding their claims moves `reclaimed`, not `failed`. The drill's second window is exactly that
   case, and it expects the budget to be spent.
2. **A rule that pages on a window with no traffic.** Five failures in twenty attempts is 25 %, and
   it is one bad minute on a queue nobody was using. Without the floor, every quiet hour pages, and
   the alert that eventually matters is the one nobody reads. The spec's last case walks both sides
   of the floor.
3. **A series that only exists after the first failure.** Writing a family into the sample only when
   a counter is non-zero is a natural optimisation and it is the one that breaks the dashboard: the
   query shows "no data" during the quiet period, which is indistinguishable from the queue being
   down. Every family is reported for every queue the deployment drains, at zero.
4. **A run id passed as a field.** It works — until somebody adds a line, a module or a task and
   does not pass it. The failure is not a compile error, it is a line that cannot be attributed, and
   it shows up months later on the one call an operator makes after an incident. A span is context;
   a field is a habit. The spec reads a real run's lines and expects the *span*, not a field.
5. **A span that does not cross a spawn.** The one-line version of the same mistake: `serve` is
   instrumented and the worker's own report has no run id at all, because `tokio::spawn` starts a
   task with an empty context — and the line that is missing is the worker's.
6. **A span built before the subscriber.** Reading the same lesson in the other direction, and the
   quieter failure of the two: `info_span!` claims its span from the dispatcher that is installed
   when it is *created*, so a span built one line too early is a span nothing ever records — the
   install line of every run arrives with no context and no error.
7. **A duration, a payload or a reason in the wrong shape.** `took = ?started.elapsed()` is a string
   like `1.2s` that a dashboard has to parse, and the payload is the customer's own record: it is
   unbounded, it is somebody's address, and it is the thing an operator finds while looking for
   something else. The line names the job; the row has everything else.
8. **A page and a drill that disagree.** The page is what a person reads at three in the morning and
   the drill is what fires the rule; if the page says 99 % and the drill measures 99.9 %, the page is
   wrong in the way that costs an afternoon. The spec compares the two numbers rather than trusting
   either.
