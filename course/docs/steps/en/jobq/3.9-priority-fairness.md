# Priority without starvation: a policy you can defend

## RECALL

- `http/rate-limit@apply` — `relay/2.13-rate-limit`: a per-tenant token bucket in front of a service,
  written to keep one caller from spending everybody else's capacity.
- `db/atomic-claim@design` — `jobq/3.7-leases-and-recovery`: the claim that takes a job atomically and
  leases it, and the order it hands jobs out in.

## DELTA

`relay/2.13` decided *whether* a caller could enter. This step decides **who waits, and for how
long** — the design half of the same question, and the half that does not show up in a benchmark.

The failure mode is worth stating plainly because it is invisible in every dashboard you would reach
for first: strict priority plus one queue that keeps refilling with high-priority work is a system
with excellent throughput and an unbounded p99 for everybody else. The workers are never idle, the
queue depth of the loud queue looks healthy, and the quiet queue's oldest job is from last Tuesday.

Two knobs, and they answer two different questions:

- **aging** — how long may a low-priority job wait? After `aging_after`, its rank rises by one level
  per interval, so a queue that floods eventually competes with the work it is flooding past. The
  wait is measured from `run_at`, the instant the job became due: a job scheduled for tomorrow is not
  being starved today, and measuring from the row's creation would starve a job nobody has asked for
  yet.
- **`max_in_flight_per_queue`** — how much of the pool may one queue hold? The *claim* enforces it,
  because claiming is where the resource is taken; jobs over the cap are not failed and not delayed,
  they stay queued and the next free slot takes them.

The ordering lives in the store — in the claim statement for the durable backend, in the sort for the
in-memory one — because the rows are chosen under `for update skip locked`: sorting the answer after
the fact would order what was already taken without changing which rows those were.

## TASK

- A `Policy` the stores are built with (`with_policy`), defaulting to what the engine did before this
  step: strict priority, no cap. Fairness is asked for, not discovered.
- Both backends apply it to the same promise: the effective rank (`priority + waited / aging`) in the
  order, and the cap in the selection — one statement on Postgres, one critical section in memory.
- The daemon wires both knobs from its settings (`worker.aging_seconds`, `worker.max_in_flight_per_queue`),
  reports them in `--check-config`, and defaults to aging on and no cap.
- The worker keeps asking *every* queue each turn: a turn is the unit of fairness across queues, and a
  queue that is asked second is not a queue that waits.

## TRAP

1. Sorting the claim's answer in Rust instead of ordering the rows in the statement — the durable
   backend takes the rows the database chose, so the sort changes the report and not the decision.
2. Aging from the row's creation time rather than from `run_at` — a job scheduled a week out competes
   with work that is due now, which is not starvation, it is a bug with a nice name.
3. Enforcing the cap by refusing to *submit* over the limit — the cap is about the pool, not about the
   queue's depth: a queue with ten thousand waiting jobs is healthy if two of them are running.
4. Marking the jobs over the cap as failed (or retryable) — they never ran. Their attempts must be
   untouched, because a retry budget spent by a full pool is a retry budget spent on nothing.
5. Making fairness the default — then every spec written before this step, and every deployment that
   depended on strict priority, changes behaviour on an upgrade. The default is the old behaviour;
   the *daemon's* default is aging on, which is a decision of the process rather than of the engine.
6. Letting the aging interval be the same number as the lease, so a job's rank and its owner's
   recovery deadline move together — two dials that look like one, and the day one of them needs to
   change it will change the other.
