# Leases, renewal and crash recovery: nobody runs a job twice by accident

## RECALL

- `db/atomic-claim@apply` — `relay/2.5-atomic-claim`: the claim is one statement, `for update skip
  locked` inside the update that stamps the rows, and the row it stamps carries a holder and an
  instant. That whole shape transfers, unchanged in spirit: `Store::claim` here stamps
  `lease_owner` and `lease_expires_at` in the same statement that counts the attempt, and the port's
  ordering promise is the one `jobq/3.5-store-port-conformance` already holds both backends to. What
  is new is what the rest of the project does with the holder — and it is the whole of this step.
- `async/graceful-shutdown@design` — `relay/2.15-shutdown-leases`: a stop is a *sequence*, and the
  interesting part of it is not the signal. A worker that is asked to stop stops **claiming**,
  finishes what is in flight inside a deadline, and hands back what the deadline cut — and the
  difference between a rollout nobody notices and one that looks like an outage is whether the row
  goes back now or in a lease. Here the sequence is the same and the process is not: there is no
  HTTP half, and the whole drain is a loop over a queue.

## DELTA

The engine can finally *run* a job — and the six things that were deferred to this step are the
step:

- **the worker loop exists.** `jobq/3.1-engine-boundary` gave the daemon its process shape and
  `jobq/3.5-store-port-conformance` gave it a durable store, and until now nothing between them did
  anything: `jobqd` announced that it was draining and waited for a signal. This step is that loop —
  claim, hand the job to a `Handler`, settle it — and the seam in `open_store` (the one that refused
  a durable URL with a sentence about a missing loop) goes away because the loop exists.
- **a claim is a lease, and the row says so.** Two columns, and every write becomes conditional on
  them. The one that matters is the *settle*: `complete` and `fail` have taken a worker name since
  `jobq/3.1-engine-boundary` and never looked at it. From this step they require the row's
  `lease_owner` to still be that worker, which is what stops the interesting accident: a worker is
  killed, its row is reclaimed and claimed by somebody else, and then the *first* worker comes back
  and settles — it has the job id, the row is running, and only the lease says it is not its
  attempt.
- **renewal is the other half of the same promise.** A handler that takes longer than a lease is not
  a bug; a lease that lapses underneath it is. The worker renews while its handler runs, one
  statement over the rows it holds, and the lease length is a *setting* (`worker.lease_seconds`)
  because the right value is the deployment's trade: longer than the slowest attempt the operator
  will wait for, shorter than the recovery they will accept.
- **the attempt nobody watched is still counted.** A crashed worker's row goes back to its queue
  with its attempts kept, and it goes to `failed` when the budget is spent — the same rule `fail`
  uses. Not counting it is how one payload that kills every worker it meets gets retried for ever:
  the budget is the only thing in the project that ends a job.
- **the operator's half is a number and a name.** `Report` counts what a run claimed, settled,
  reclaimed and handed back, and the daemon logs them on the way out; the row keeps the name of
  whoever holds it, which is the thing an operator greps for at three in the morning. This step is
  the first in the track where the *answer to an incident* is a row and two settings rather than a
  stack trace.
- **clock skew is why the instant decides, not the owner.** The lease's end is computed from the
  engine's clock and bound into the statement (`jobq/3.8-clock-injection` is where that clock
  becomes injectable, and where this file's `Utc::now()` calls get a seam). The reclaimer's predicate
  is `lease_expires_at <= now`, so a worker whose clock is a second fast does not lose its row to a
  worker whose clock is a second slow: the row carries the instant the *owner* wrote, and everybody
  compares against it.

## TASK

The scaffold added two files, and everything else is an edit to files you already own.

### `migrations/0003_leases.sql`

Two columns on `jobs`: `lease_owner text` and `lease_expires_at timestamptz`, both null when nobody
holds the job. The file is yours; two things about it are decisions:

- the reclaimer's predicate is `state = 'running' and lease_expires_at <= now()`, which is the query
  every worker runs at the start of every turn — that is the one worth an index, and it should be
  partial like `jobs_claimable`;
- nothing else changes: a lease is a column pair, not a state.

### `src/service/worker.rs`

The scaffold shipped the vocabulary — `Handler`, `WorkerSettings`, `Shutdown`, `Report`, `Worker` —
and one `todo!()`: the loop. Implement it as the turn it describes:

1. **claim** up to `batch` jobs from every configured queue, and count them;
2. **handle each in its own task**, and settle it where it finishes: `complete` on success, `fail`
   with the handler's own words on failure. Settle through `crate::engine::retry` with
   `DEFAULT_ATTEMPTS` — a settle runs at an isolation level that can be refused
   (`jobq/3.6-transaction-isolation`), and the retry is the caller's half of that decision;
3. **renew** the leases this worker holds, on a ticker beside the work (a renewal at half the lease,
   so two may be lost before the claim lapses), and log a renewal that fails: it is the only warning
   an operator gets before the same job runs twice;
4. **reclaim** what a worker that is no longer here left behind, every turn, before claiming: this
   is the line that makes a crash a delay rather than a lost job;
5. **drain**: the signal ends the claiming; the work in flight gets `shutdown` to finish; what the
   deadline cuts is aborted and handed back, and the report says how much of each.

### `src/service/handler.rs`

One program per attempt, its payload on standard input, its exit status as the verdict (zero is
done, anything else is a failed attempt whose reason is that status). Two decisions are worth
stating: the program's output is *inherited* rather than captured (so a handler cannot deadlock the
daemon by filling a pipe), and a child that is still running when the drain deadline passes is
killed by a `Drop` — dropping a task runs no cleanup of its own, so the kill has to be where the
value goes out of scope. The daemon's default handler, when `worker.handler` is not set, records the
job and settles it as succeeded.

### `src/engine/store/`

The port grows the methods the loop needs, and every one of them is a promise both backends keep:

- `migrate` — because the daemon now starts a durable store on every start, and the fixture of
  `jobq/3.5-store-port-conformance` has been calling this method's implementation since then. The
  in-memory store answers `Ok(())`.
- `renew(worker)` — extend the leases *this* worker holds (and nothing else: a worker that could
  renew anybody's claim could keep an attempt it does not own alive for ever).
- `release(worker)` — hand back what this worker still holds, with its attempts kept and its lease
  released. This is what a drain deadline uses.
- `reclaim_expired()` — every row whose lease has run out goes back to `queued`, or to `failed` when
  its budget is spent, with the attempt kept and a `reclaimed` line in its trail.

The durable backend writes each of the last two as **one statement** (a data-modifying `with` that
moves the rows and inserts their trail lines together), for the reason
`jobq/3.6-transaction-isolation` gives: the decision and the line that describes it are one write or
they are a lie. `claim` stamps the lease in the statement that counts the attempt; `complete` and
`fail` add `and lease_owner = $worker` to their guard.

### `src/engine/domain.rs`, `src/service/config.rs`, `src/service/mod.rs`

- `Job` gains `lease_owner()` and `lease_expires_at()` — the row has to be *readable* through the
  port, or the columns are only visible to SQL (the field list grows, the accessors do not change
  what was there, and that is what `#[non_exhaustive]` was for).
- `worker.lease_seconds` (default 60, zero refused) and `worker.handler` (a program and its
  arguments; empty refused), both reported by `--check-config` on their own lines.
- `serve` opens the store with that lease, migrates it when `store.migrate` says so, builds the
  handler, runs the worker, and turns `SIGTERM` into the worker's own signal. The `draining` line
  and the exit code are unchanged — `jobq/3.1-engine-boundary` pins both, and it prints the line
  *after* the worker is running so that the word means what it says.

## TRAP

1. **A settle that only checks `state = 'running'`.** Every case about the *happy* path passes, and
   then a worker that was killed, reclaimed and replaced comes back and settles somebody else's
   attempt: the row is running, the id matches, and nothing in the guard says "not yours".
   `a_late_worker_cannot_settle_a_job_it_no_longer_holds` fails with `expected Error::Invalid, got
   Ok(())`, and the ledger it protects is the difference between one delivery and two.
2. **Forgetting to renew.** The handler is slower than the lease, so the row's instant passes while
   it is still being handled — and the next worker finds an "abandoned" row, moves it back and claims
   it: one job, two attempts, two handlers, and no line in the log that says so. The renewal case
   fails with `the row went back to Queued while its worker was still handling it`.
3. **Renewing everything instead of what you hold.** `renew` without the `lease_owner = this worker`
   filter keeps *another* worker's claim alive for ever, which hides exactly the crash this step is
   for — and it is the same mistake as `release` without the filter, which puts an attempt that is
   running right now back in the queue for a third worker to take.
4. **A reclaimer that only moves rows back.** Half the promise is the attempt count: a row that
   comes back without its attempt counted is a payload that kills its worker, retried until somebody
   notices. The other half is the budget: a reclaim that always answers `queued` never lets a job
   die, so a poison payload is immortal. `a_job_whose_attempts_are_spent_is_not_handed_out_again`
   watches a job die without anybody failing it.
5. **A drain that waits for the lease.** Correct and slow: every rollout hides the jobs that were in
   flight for the length of a lease, so a deploy that should cost seconds looks like an outage.
   `release` on the way out is what turns that into `the job went back to the queue` —
   `the_drain_deadline_hands_the_job_back` is the case, and the assertion has *no owner* in it.
6. **Claiming while draining.** A loop that keeps turning while it waits for the drain takes on work
   it will not finish. The signal has to end the *claiming*, not just the turn:
   `a_signal_drains_without_claiming_again` writes a job immediately after the signal and expects it
   still `queued` with no attempt spent.
7. **Waiting for the child process instead of polling it.** The daemon has to be able to give up on a
   handler at the drain deadline, so the wait has to be interruptible (`try_wait` in a loop, or a
   `tokio` process with a kill-on-drop guard). A blocking `wait_with_output` in the handler makes the
   drain deadline a suggestion: `the_drain_deadline_hands_the_job_back` then hangs until the script
   the case wrote finishes, which is thirty seconds of a test that should take two.
8. **Building the store and the worker with two different leases.** The store stamps the instant and
   the worker renews before it; a deployment that sets them separately is a deployment whose
   renewals arrive too late, and the only symptom is case 2's. The daemon has one setting
   (`worker.lease_seconds`) and passes it to both for that reason.
