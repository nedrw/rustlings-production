# A performance budget that a slow machine cannot fail

## WHY

"It got slower" is the review comment nobody can act on, and the obvious answer to it — time the
thing — is a measurement of the machine that ran it. A wall-clock threshold on a shared build host is
red on a busy afternoon and green on a quiet one, and the reflex it teaches is to re-run the job until
it passes. A gate nobody believes is a gate nobody keeps.

What a machine cannot change is the *work*: the rows a loop looked at, the writes it made, how much of
that grew when the input doubled. Those are numbers a review can read, a learner can run locally
before pushing, and a gate can compare against a baseline somebody measured on purpose — and the last
of them is the one that matters most, because a small input hides a quadratic algorithm completely:
the drain that keeps its place pays sixteen cells for a queue of sixteen, the drain written to start
its search over on every row pays a hundred and twelve, and both finish before a reader can look up.

This step builds that budget out of three things: a counter, a guard over the counter's shape, and the
names and bounds a harness prints so that a gate — and a reader — can compare them.

## RECALL

- `test/coverage-policy@apply` — `jobq/3.21-coverage-policy`: the policy half of proving quality, and
  the step this one follows. Coverage says which places a suite *reached*; this step measures what the
  code *costs* while it is reached. Both are claims written down where a reviewer can argue with the
  numbers, and neither is a measurement of the machine. **This step does not re-teach the policy.**
  What is new is the counter, the guard over its shape, and the numbers a harness prints.
- `db/atomic-claim@design` — `jobq/3.7-leases-and-recovery`: `claim` is where the queue's work lives —
  one lock, select and stamp in the same critical section — and it is the operation a budget on this
  engine is about. The drain this step measures is the same batch a claim hands a worker, in the order
  the store ranked it, which is the order `jobq/3.9-priority-fairness` decides.

## DELTA

Three names and one file:

- **`Work`** — two counters, not one. Cells examined and statements written answer different
  questions, and a defect shows in exactly one of them: a drain that looks at every row twice has
  twice the cells and the same statements, one that hands the same row out twice is the opposite. A
  single total would only say that something changed.
- **`scale_to`** — the guard. `work(2n) / work(n)`: about two for a drain whose cost follows the
  queue, about four per doubling for one that rescans from the front — its cells per row grow with
  the queue (97 at 256 rows, against one) — and `None` when the smaller run did no work at all,
  because a comparison with no denominator is not a number.
- **`Row`, `Pull`** — a batch in the order a store ranked it and the drain that keeps its place. The
  cursor is the whole of the difference between the two drains above, and it is the decision the step
  is about.
- **`METRICS`, `SCALE_MAX`, `report`** — the project's half of a budget a *gate* holds it to. The
  course keeps the baseline (`course/tracks/jobq/budget/3.22-bench-budget.toml`), the scaffold ships
  the harness that prints these lines, and the names are one list read from both sides.

## TASK

The scaffold added a module, a harness and a page. **Nothing declares the module yet**: wiring
`src/engine/mod.rs` is your first decision, and until it is made neither the spec nor the harness
compiles.

### `Work` — what one operation cost

`examine` per row looked at, `statement` per row handed out, and `total` for the pair. `scale_to`
answers how much more the larger measurement cost — and `None` rather than `0.0` when there is nothing
to divide by, because a zero compares with the bound like any other ratio and would report a *pass*
for a drain that measured nothing.

### `Row` and `Pull` — a batch, and a drain that remembers

A row is the identity a worker is handed and the instant it may run; `is_due` is `<=`, because a job
scheduled for this instant is due at this instant. The pull keeps its cursor: a row it hands out is
behind it for good, and a row that is not due at the `now` it was asked with is stepped over rather
than asked about again — this is a drain, not a timer, and the batch arrived from a store that had
already filtered by the instant it was asked about. That is what makes a drain cost one cell per row
instead of the square.

### `METRICS`, `SCALE_MAX` and `report` — the numbers somebody else reads

The three metric names, the bound the guard is judged against, and the lines a harness prints from a
pair of measurements. The counts come from the smaller of the two and the guard from the pair, and a
pair with no denominator prints two lines: the gate that reads them treats a missing metric as
`blocked` rather than as a pass, which is the honest answer when the instrument measured nothing.

### `docs/budget.md` — the baseline, written down

The stub names the workload and the shape. Fill it in: what each metric counts, the baseline the
course ships for it, why the guard is an upper bound rather than a target, and how the gate compares
them. The spec reads this page and compares it with `METRICS` and `SCALE_MAX`.

### What deliberately does not change

The harness (`tests/budget.rs`) is the course's file: it is the instrument, and a learner editing the
instrument is a learner editing the measurement. The workload's size is content too — changing it
changes what every baseline means, so it moves in the same commit as the numbers.

## TRAP

1. **Timing it.** A budget measured in milliseconds is a budget the machine decides, and the gate
   that reads it goes red when somebody else's build was running. `Work` counts cells and statements
   for exactly this reason: the numbers are the same in every process, on every machine, in every
   run.
2. **A workload that is entirely due.** Then `scan_cells` equals `claim_statements` and the drain has
   nothing to look at and skip: the pair says nothing the single count did not, and the guard is
   measuring a loop with no decisions in it. One row in four is what makes the two counts different
   facts.
3. **A short queue.** The drain that starts over is 112 cells on a sixteen-row batch against the
   honest drain's 16, and 3.7 times the work when the batch doubles — a difference, but one a
   reviewer skimming a green gate will not see on a four-row fixture, where the two are 10 and 4.
   The guard exists because the ratio is the part that does *not* need a long queue to be meaningful.
4. **A `_max` metric read as a target.** `scale_ratio_max = 2.2` is an upper bound: a drain that got
   cheaper is a drain that passed, and a change that brings the reference ratio down to 1.9 does not
   need the baseline edited. Treating it as "about 2.2" turns a guard into a target somebody tunes.
5. **Changing the workload and keeping the numbers.** The baselines are counts for a 256-row queue
   with one row in four not due; a harness measured on 64 rows prints a different `scan_cells` and the
   same `scale_ratio_max`, and the ±10% comparison against a baseline nobody re-measured is a gate
   that fails for a reason the page does not explain.
6. **Printing `0` for a ratio with no denominator.** `scale_ratio_max=0` is a *pass* for a drain that
   measured nothing — the one answer a budget must never give by accident. `scale_to` answers `None`
   and `report` leaves the line out, which the gate reports as `blocked`.
7. **Editing the harness or the baseline instead of the code.** `tests/budget.rs` is the course's
   file and `course/tracks/jobq/budget/3.22-bench-budget.toml` is the copy the gate reads — editing
   either changes the instrument, not the machine it measures. `kiln reset jobq/3.22-bench-budget`
   puts the harness back.
