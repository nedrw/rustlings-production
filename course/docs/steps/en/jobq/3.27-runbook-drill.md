# Run the runbook: a drill that finds the operator's missing step

## RECALL

- `ops/runbook-drill@apply` — `mailer/1.22-runbook`: **the shape this step deepens, and the two
  lessons it keeps.** One failure, replayed end to end; the drill reports the promise (nothing was
  lost) and is run twice, once with the broken thing coming back and once with it never coming back,
  because the second run is what makes the first one evidence. The page is written *from* the drill
  so the two cannot drift apart, and its five sections — Symptoms, Signals, Steps, Verification,
  Escalation — are the five questions an incident asks.
- `obs/metrics-slo@design` — `jobq/3.17-error-budget-alerts`: **the signals a stage is read against,
  and the one thing about them that matters at three in the morning.** That step wrote the rules
  down as *signal, threshold, window, drill* and made the sample line the unit a window is made of.
  What it also decided, in the daemon, is that a run publishes its readings on the way out: the
  numbers exist per queue and per report, and the command that reads them *now* is a command rather
  than a stream.
- `ops/dlq-tooling@operate` — `jobq/3.26-dlq-cli`: **the acts this procedure's middle stages take.**
  One identity, one act, one answer, one code; a refused act writes nothing and moves nothing; a
  replay is reversible and a discard is not, which is why the discard demands a reason and a name.
  That step is where those promises were *made*; this one is where they are written into a
  procedure and replayed.
- `db/atomic-claim@design` (`jobq/3.7-leases-and-recovery`) and
  `async/graceful-shutdown@operate` (`jobq/3.7`) are not in `requires`, but two stages lean on them
  by name: a row a departed worker holds comes back when its lease runs out, and a stop that
  outlives its drain deadline **hands its rows back** rather than holding them.

## DELTA

Twenty-six steps produced acts. Each one shipped the page that explains its own act — the schema's
procedure (`docs/migration.md`), the alerting rules, the dead-letter door, the release — and **not
one of them shipped the procedure**: the order a responder takes those acts in, written for somebody
who did not write any of them. Four pages of *what this tool does* are not a runbook, and the way to
find out is to run one: a procedure that has never been executed is a list of wishes, and the stage
it is missing is the one nobody notices until the incident that needs it.

So this step changes what the deliverable *is*. The page is no longer prose about a subsystem; it is
the procedure, and it is **data** — `Runbook::of` reads it, the drill walks it, and the harness
counts it. Three decisions follow from that, and each one is a way a written procedure fails:

- **a stage owes four things, and the fourth is the one nobody writes.** The command, the signal
  that says it answered well, the refusal that says it did not, and the rollback that gets the queue
  back. `Runbook::gaps` refuses a stage that does not name all four, and the reason the refusal and
  the rollback are in the same list as the command is that they are the two a responder needs
  *during* the incident rather than after it: an act whose refusal is a sentence is an act that gets
  retried into the same refusal, and an act that cannot be undone has to say so **before** it is
  taken. Every page this track has shipped so far names commands and signals; none of them names a
  rollback at all, and the drill is what notices (`grep -ri rollback docs/*.md` finds the word once,
  in `scrollback`).
- **the drill walks the page rather than describing it.** `tests/support/drill.rs` reads
  `docs/runbook.md`, takes the act each stage names **in the order the page names them**, and reports
  what was left owed. The two are compared in both directions: an act the page names and the drill
  cannot take is a stage nobody has run, and an act the drill knows and the page does not name is the
  stage this step exists to find (`Report::unnamed`). That is what makes "runnable by somebody who
  did not write it" a property rather than a hope — the drill is that somebody, and it has no memory
  of the incident.
- **a drill that cannot fail is decoration.** Three worlds, and the last two are the falsifications:
  `Recovers` is the promise; `Never` is the same procedure with nobody left to take the queue on,
  which reports the work still owed; and `Refused` takes the act that cannot be undone **before** the
  stage that says which rows it is for — the moment the rollback column has to be right.

**What the drill finds that the track had missing** — and it is the reason the page has to be walked
rather than written in one sitting: the acts on the dead letters. Stage 3 says to look at the rows;
the acts that *change* them are the two stages nothing in this project's pages has ever put in a
procedure. The replay is named wherever 3.16 is explained, and the discard is named in the door's
help text — but a responder reading the accumulated pages finds *what to look at* and never *what to
do*, and the drill says so out loud: an act it takes and the page does not name comes out of the
report as `unnamed`, with the command it needed. The pair of worlds that follows is the second half
of the same finding: a refused act leaves the queue exactly where it was (so the repair is the call
and not the queue), and the discard has **none** in its rollback column, which is the one word that
has to be on the page before anybody points that act at a row.

Two names are added to the engine, and both are about reading a page rather than writing one:
`Runbook::of(page) -> Runbook` parses the numbered stages out of a markdown page, and
`Runbook::gaps() -> Vec<Gap>` answers every stage that is not runnable — empty, incomplete, or
holding a place its number does not mean. What is *not* here: anything that judges whether the acts
work. The engine's own suite is where an act is proved, and a page is not the place a queue is
fixed.

## TASK

The scaffold added four things and wired none of them: `src/engine/runbook.rs` is the reader with its
judgement left out, `docs/runbook.md` is the page with five sections and five unfinished stages,
`tests/support/drill.rs` is the drill that sets the incident up and reports what it arranged to
happen, and `tests/runbook_harness.rs` is the harness `slow:bench-budget@10` runs.

### `src/engine/runbook.rs` — the reader, and where it is declared

**Nothing declares the new module yet**, and that is your first decision: `src/engine/mod.rs` needs
`pub(crate) mod runbook;` and the re-export of the four public names (`Gap`, `PARTS`, `Runbook`,
`Stage`). Until it is made, neither the spec nor the harness compiles — which is the failure
`kiln run jobq/3.27-runbook-drill` shows you first. The module is `pub(crate)`, not `pub`, for the
reason `jobq/3.2-public-api-baseline` gave: what a caller can name is what `mod.rs` re-exports.

Three bodies are yours, and the rest of the file — the types, the accessors, the `Display`
impl — is scaffolding you keep:

- `Stage::missing()` answers the first of `PARTS` this stage does not name. "Does not name"
  includes naming it and answering nothing: `- rollback:` is the shape of a responder who was about
  to think about it, and it is the same gap as a stage that never mentions a rollback;
- `Runbook::of(page)` is the parse. A stage is a `###` heading that carries a number; a part is a
  line that reads `- command: …` (the four names in `PARTS`) and it continues onto the wrapped
  lines under it until a blank line, a heading, or the next part. A `###` heading **without** a
  number is not a stage — it is a heading where a stage belongs, and the acts it was meant to name
  are exactly the ones the drill reports as unnamed;
- `Runbook::gaps()` is the judgement: `Empty` for a page with no stage at all, `Missing` for a stage
  that forgets a part, `OutOfOrder` for a stage whose number is not the place it holds. Both are
  reported for the same stage when both are true, because they are two different repairs.

The two free functions at the bottom (`numbered`, `named`) are the line readers the parse needs, and
nothing else in the crate may call them: they are the detail a page's syntax is made of.

### `docs/runbook.md` — the page

The five sections are there (`mailer/1.22-runbook`'s, in the order a responder reads them), the
`## Steps` section is where a stage lives, and the page names the drill a responder replays. Three
jobs:

1. **finish the stages that are there.** Each of the five names its command, its signal, its
   refusal and its rollback, and each one names a class this deployment answers and one of the
   signals `jobq/3.17-error-budget-alerts` publishes. The page has the exit-code table of
   `docs/dlq.md` to read them off;
2. **add the stages the drill finds.** Run `cargo test -p jobq --test spec_runbook` once the module
   is wired and read the `unnamed` list: the acts it names are the stages the page is missing. They
   are the two the incident is *about* — putting a dead letter back, and throwing one away — and the
   page has to hold them in the order they belong in, before the rollout;
3. **write the rollback column of the act that cannot be undone** in the word this step holds it to:
   the discard's rollback is `none`, because there is no way back from it, and the page says so where
   somebody reads it before acting rather than after.

The `links` gate runs over this page and every other markdown file in the project: relative targets
have to exist, `#anchors` have to match a heading, and a `TODO` where a target should be is a
failure. The spec checks what a gate cannot: that every stage names the four things, that the page
names the signals and the classes, and that the drill can replay it.

### `tests/support/drill.rs` — the drill

It already sets the incident up and reports a `Report` it decided rather than one it measured: four
jobs accepted, one worker claiming all four, two attempts spending their budget, one job owed
another, and one row still held by the worker that is about to be stopped. What it does not do is
ask the page anything, and `World::Never` and `World::Refused` answer what `World::Recovers` answers.
Turning it into a drill means four things, in this order:

1. **compare the page and the script before taking a single act.** The acts this file knows
   (`ACTS`) and the stages the page names are one list read from both ends; what the page does not
   name is the finding, and it is reported rather than skipped;
2. **walk the stages in the page's order** and take each one's act. A stage whose command is not one
   of the acts is drift between the page and the evidence, and the drill says so with the command it
   could not take — not with a skipped stage;
3. **check the page's own claims as you go.** That is what a drill is for: the queue really is stuck
   (`claimed` above `succeeded`), one row really is `running`, two dead letters really are there,
   and the act that hands back what a daemon holds really moves a row;
4. **report what is still owed at the end**, and let the world decide it: `Recovers` is a worker
   taking the queue on afterwards, `Never` is nobody doing so, and `Refused` is the same procedure
   with one act taken too early — the refused act writes nothing, which the drill asserts rather
   than assumes.

The drill runs on a clock the file states and a store that needs no container, so the whole replay
is milliseconds. **The spec runs it several times**, and `slow:mutants@80` runs the spec a dozen
times more: an incident that has to be waited out is an incident that makes every one of those runs
slower for nothing.

**What this drill is honest about**: the refusal it replays is the *door's* own — an act pointed at a
row that is not a dead letter, answered with class `2` (`jobq/3.26-dlq-cli`). A database that refuses
the write is the other refusal, class `4`, and the in-memory store cannot be made to produce it; what
the page keeps from that one is the line, because the class is what a responder branches on and both
classes have the same consequence for the procedure: nothing was written, so the repair is the call
and not the queue. A deployment with a durable backend is where the class-4 path is real, and
`jobq/3.26-dlq-cli`'s own spec is where it is driven through the real binary.

### `tests/runbook_harness.rs` — the course's instrument

It reads `docs/runbook.md`, asks the crate's reader for the stages, and prints four counts —
`jobq-budget acts=6`, `named=6`, `unnamed_max=0`, `unrunnable_max=0` — which `slow:bench-budget@10`
compares with `course/tracks/jobq/budget/3.27-runbook-drill.toml`. Counts of acts and stages, never
seconds, so the gate answers the same thing on a loaded machine as on an idle one. The file is the
course's, not yours: editing it edits the measurement, and `kiln reset jobq/3.27-runbook-drill` puts
it back.

### What the spec judges

Nine cases in three groups, and every row below is a claim you can run:

| the case | the claim |
| --- | --- |
| `the_page_is_a_procedure_whose_stages_all_hold` | the page has no placeholder left, names at least five stages, and every stage answers all four of `PARTS` — judged through the crate's own reader |
| `the_page_names_its_sections_the_drill_and_the_signals` | the five sections, the drill a responder replays (`spec_runbook`), the signals of `jobq/3.17`, and the classes a script branches on |
| `the_drill_runs_every_stage_the_page_names` | the drill takes every act the page names, in the page's order, with nothing unnamed, and the promise the last stage makes holds when a worker comes back |
| `the_drill_reports_the_work_that_is_still_owed` | the same procedure in a world where nobody comes back reports the work still owed — and the promise is broken there, which is what makes the other run evidence |
| `the_drill_replays_the_refusal_and_the_page_says_what_it_leaves` | the refused act is walked, the stage it belongs to is on the page, its refusal names class `2`, its rollback says `none`, and the rest of the procedure is unchanged |
| `a_stage_that_does_not_name_its_four_parts_is_a_gap` | one case per part, plus a part named with nothing after it: a page this file writes, so a reader that waves a page through fails here |
| `a_stage_holds_the_place_its_number_says` | 1, 2, 3 is a procedure; a repeated number, a skipped one and a page that starts at 2 are not, and an out-of-order stage that is also incomplete reports both |
| `a_page_that_names_no_stage_is_prose` | a page with no numbered stage is `Empty`, an unnumbered `###` heading is not a stage, and a `####` heading is prose inside one |
| `a_part_wrapped_onto_the_next_line_is_read_whole` | a page written at a readable width parses whole, and a blank line ends the part rather than extending it |

## TRAP

1. **A page that is prose with numbers in front of it.** Five stages that say "look at the dead
   letters" and never say which command, what a good answer looks like, what a refusal means, or how
   to get back are the shape of a runbook that is read once and abandoned. `Runbook::gaps` names the
   stage and the part, and the harness counts them: `unnamed_max` and `unrunnable_max` are both zero
   on a procedure somebody can follow. The failure this prevents is specific — the responder who
   reads "replay the dead letters" and types `jobqc dlq replay <ID>` without `--by` gets class `2`
   and a refusal message, and a column that never said a name was required sends them to the source.
2. **A drill that reports the promise held in every world.** Two worlds that differ in nothing are
   one drill run twice; `Never` and `Refused` are what make the `Recovers` run evidence, and the
   `Report` below is what a drill that decided its own answer looks like — `stages` counted from the
   page rather than walked, `unnamed` empty because nothing was compared, and `promise_held()`
   answering `true` for a queue nobody has touched.
3. **A refused act treated as a failure to undo.** Nothing was written, so there is nothing to roll
   back: the repair is the call. A procedure whose refusal column says "try again" is a procedure
   that teaches a responder to retry a call that will always be refused — and the drill asserts the
   strong form (`the rows are exactly where they were`, `no line in the record`), so a door that
   started writing something on the way to a refusal fails here rather than in production.
4. **The discard given a rollback it does not have.** It is the one act on this page that cannot be
   undone, and a page that lists a rollback for it is worse than one that lists nothing: the
   responder takes it believing there is a way back. The word is `none`, and the reason the act
   demands `--by` and `--reason` is that the record's line is the whole of what survives.
5. **A stage numbered by hand and reordered by hand.** A procedure is followed in the page's order,
   and `OutOfOrder` is the objection: a repeated number, a skipped one, or a page that starts at 2
   is a page whose steps are not where their numbers say. The parse does not sort and does not
   renumber — a page is read by a person under pressure, so what it says is what it says.
6. **Waiting for a lease inside the drill.** The lease is a real repair and it costs
   `worker.lease_seconds` — a number that belongs in a stage's signal, not in a test. A drill that
   sleeps for it is a drill the spec runs a dozen times under `slow:mutants@80`, which is minutes of
   the suite spent watching a clock. The clock here is frozen at an instant the file states, and the
   procedure's own stage is the fast repair.
7. **Editing the harness or the baseline instead of the page.** `tests/runbook_harness.rs` is the
   course's file and `course/tracks/jobq/budget/3.27-runbook-drill.toml` is the copy the gate reads:
   editing either changes the instrument, not the thing it measures. The page is the deliverable.
8. **A stage whose command is not this deployment's.** The drill takes the acts of `ACTS`, and a
   stage naming an orchestrator's rollback (`kubectl rollout restart`) is drift: the drill has no
   act for it and says which command it could not take. A runbook for a deployment names the
   binaries and the signals of that deployment, or it is a document about an imagined one.
