# Properties over examples: invariants a random walk keeps

## WHY

Every case this project has written so far is an example: submit these two jobs, claim one, assert
the row. An example is a decision somebody made about which inputs are interesting, and the inputs
nobody thought of are exactly where the defects live. The suite goes on passing while the code
changes underneath it, because it was never asked the question the change broke.

There is a second cost, and it is the one this step is about. A rule you cannot *ask* is a rule you
cannot check. `jobq/3.14-job-state-machine` turned the life of a job into one total function for
exactly this reason, and its own documentation promised the walk that this step writes: the states
and the events are lists, and every answer is a call, so "is there an operation sequence that ends
somewhere it must not?" is a question a program can ask. What is missing is the machine that asks
it a few hundred times and, when the answer is no, hands back the one case where it is.

The half that makes a property worth writing is therefore not the property: it is the
**counter-example** — a seed and the operations that break it. Without it, a red build says "this
queue is broken somewhere" to somebody who has to reproduce it by hand. With it, the incident is a
line in a CI log, and the fix starts with a test that already exists.

## TASK

The scaffold added one module and **nothing declares it yet**: wiring `src/engine/mod.rs` the way
`jobq/3.13-queue-authz-attribution` wired `authz`, `tenant` and `usage` is your first decision, and
until it is made the spec does not compile. Four decisions follow from the shapes in the file.

### The generator is a seed, and the seed is the whole state

`Rng` turns one `u64` into the operation sequence a walk plays. The arithmetic is integer
arithmetic and nothing else — no clock, no thread id, no address, no hash seed — because a finding
that cannot be replayed is a rumour, and what a CI log needs to be worth anything is that a
colleague can produce the same failure on their machine from the number in the message. `splitmix64`
is the implementation the module documentation argues for: a counter and two mixing steps.

`Rng::below(bound)` is the only thing the walk asks of it, and it has two edges. It answers inside
its range (a walk that drew an index outside the alphabet would be walking something that is not the
model), and it answers *something* when there is nothing to draw from: a remainder is not perfectly
uniform when the bound does not divide 2^64, and that trade is made on purpose, because a walk is a
search for a counter-example and not a sample for a statistic.

### The invariant is asked after every step

`Invariant::holds` is handed the history and the step that just happened, and the walk stops at the
*first* step that says no. Both halves of that sentence are decisions:

- **the step, not the state.** "A finished job is never handed out again" is a sentence about a
  before and an after; a checker that saw only the state would have to keep a copy of the trajectory
  to say it, which is the second implementation this step exists to avoid;
- **the history, not just the last event.** Two completions in one walk is a property about what the
  queue *saw*, and a refusal belongs in it: the event happened and the machine said no.

A refusal is a **step**: `transition` refuses sixteen of the twenty-four pairs, so a walker that
stopped (or dropped the event) at the first refusal would only ever see the moves a job is meant to
make — and every property about the end of a job would hold because nothing ever got there.

### The model is the engine's table, and a case is replayable

`Machine` is `transition`, not a second copy of it: a property over a copy is a test of the copy.
The `Model` trait exists for the other direction — a walk pointed at a rule somebody *changed on
purpose*, which is exactly what `jobq/3.20-mutation-threshold` does with a table of them — and for
that to mean anything, the unchanged model has to be the real one.

`walk` generates and plays; `replay` plays a case somebody wrote down; `shrink` takes out what the
failure does not need. The contract of `shrink` is three sentences, and the spec checks all three:
the answer still breaks the invariant, its operations are the ones it was given in the same order
with some left out, and **no single operation can be taken out of the answer any more**. What it
must not be is "the smallest": that is a search over every subset, and the case a person reads is
the one where nothing obvious is left to remove.

### What deliberately does not change

The table (`transition`), the alphabet (`STATES`/`EVENTS`, which a walk draws from rather than
re-declaring), the model's refusals, and every earlier suite. The alphabet is also why this walk has
no event for the clock: `sched/delayed-timers@apply` — `jobq/3.11-delayed-timers` makes a `queued`
row *due* and never moves it between states, because being due is a property of the row and not a
state it is in. A walk over the model is a walk over the states; the clock is the store's business.
If a `DONE WHEN` line below fails, the harness and the engine disagree — and *that* is the defect
this step is shaped to catch.

  defect found and replayed, and shrinking.
- `kiln check jobq/3.19-property-invariants` — every must gate is green.
- `kiln check jobq/3.14-job-state-machine` — the table still answers the same way: this step added a
  way of asking it, not a second rule.

## TRAP

1. **Asking the invariant at the end of the walk.** It is one line shorter and it produces a
   counter-example made of every operation the walk played: the case that failed is buried in the
   noise a random walk added after it. The spec hands the walk an invariant that cannot hold and
   expects *one* operation back.
2. **A generator that is not a function of its seed.** `SystemTime::now()`, `RandomState`, a
   `HashMap`'s iteration order, a thread id — each of them makes the walk different every run, and
   the seed in the message then reproduces nothing. Two walks from one seed are one walk; the spec
   compares them.
3. **Stopping at the first refusal.** It reads like tolerance and it is the opposite: the walk never
   reaches `failed` or `succeeded`, and the property about terminal jobs passes for the wrong
   reason. The spec walks eight seeds of forty steps and checks that all four states were reached.
4. **A `Model` that re-states the table.** It agrees with `transition` today, and the day one of the
   two is edited is the day the property starts testing the copy. The spec replays every step the
   walk produced through `transition` and compares.
5. **Dropping a refused event from the history.** The property then talks about what the queue did
   rather than about what arrived, and the case with a model that refuses everything is where that
   shows.
6. **`shrink` that stops at the first shorter sequence.** "Shorter" is not the contract: the case
   has to be one where no single operation can be removed and the failure survives, and the spec
   checks that by removing each one itself.
