# Write amplification: batch the calls, and count what a flush costs

## WHY

Every append this store has ever taken opened the log, wrote one record and dropped the handle —
which is honest about what an append *costs*, and the reason this step exists. Three calls sit in
that path, and only the last one is about the disk:

- a **device flush** is milliseconds. `lakelet/4.3-write-path-adr` measured one at 4.1 ms, which is
  why this build flushes on an interval instead of per record — a store that waits per record cannot
  exceed about 240 records/s however fast the disk is;
- an **open** walks the path, takes the inode lock and allocates a descriptor;
- a **write** copies the bytes into the page cache.

The last two are microseconds, and that is exactly why they are the budget: they are paid per
*call*, not per byte. Sixty-four records handed to the kernel in one piece cost one open and one
write; handed over one at a time they cost a hundred and twenty-eight calls, for the same on-disk
bytes in the same order. That ratio has a name — **write amplification**, plus the calls per record —
and the point of this step is that the ratio is a number the write path counts, prints and stays
within, instead of an impression somebody has to argue about.

## RECALL

- `store/wal-recovery@apply — lakelet/4.4-wal-recovery`: a record is `[length][payload][seal]` behind
  a log's header, replay stops at the first record it cannot prove, and a store is recovered by
  opening it. This step writes *many* of those records in one call, and the format is not allowed to
  notice: a batch has to be the bytes N appends write, byte for byte, so a reader or a recovery can
  still see N records and a cut inside the batch still recovers to a prefix of it.
- `async/backpressure@aware — mailer/1.14-worker`: a queue with a bound and a decision about what to
  drop when it fills. This step is the other side of that question — it makes the write path's cost per
  call small and lets a turn's frames share one call — and it deliberately does **not** bound how much
  arrives in a turn. `lakelet/4.6-ingest-backpressure` is where the bound and the drop policy land,
  and the batch size becomes a decision with a queue in front of it.

## TASK

Four properties make up this step. Everything else is packaging.

- **A batch changes the calls, and nothing else.** `Spool::append_batch` writes N payloads as N
  records — in the order given, in `wal::segment`'s format — with **one open and one write**, plus one
  device call when the policy asks for one. `Spool::append` becomes a batch of one, so there is a
  single write path and every earlier promise holds through it. Say in the page which syscall count
  went down and which bytes did not: they are the two halves of the invariant the spec checks by
  writing the same payloads into two stores and comparing the files.
- **An acknowledgement may never move ahead of the write.** That is `lakelet/4.1-ingest-protocol`'s
  promise, and batching does not weaken it: the frames one turn of the accept loop decoded are
  written as one batch *before* any of them is counted as accepted. A batcher that answered first
  and wrote afterwards — on a timer, on the next turn, from a background task — is the defect this
  step must not introduce, and it is invisible in any test that only asks what ended up on the file.
  The spec drives the daemon for exactly this: a burst of frames in one write, and the log read the
  moment the daemon says the connection is closed.
- **The counters are the lesson, and they are observable.** `src/engine/flush/batcher.rs` holds
  `Stats`: records written, bytes written, **calls** made (the opens included), device flushes, and
  batches. `Spool::stats()` answers them, `tests/budget.rs` prints them as `lakelet-budget
  <name>=<value>` lines, and the daemon prints them at a stop (`store write cost records=… bytes=…
  batches=… calls=… syncs=…`). A counter is charged **where the call happens** — the open and the
  write in `append_batch`, the flush in `Spool::sync` — because a counter the caller increments
  reports what the caller believes it did.
- **Write amplification is a number, not a feeling.** Bytes written per payload byte, and calls per
  record, both taken at two batch sizes so a cost that follows the workload cannot hide. Nothing in
  this step is judged by a *time*: the budget lane compares counts (`handoff M2 §1.1`), and a number
  that needs a stopwatch to be trusted is not a number this project puts in a gate.

**What the course gives you, and what is yours.** The scaffold declares the shape, because the
counters are a contract other steps read:

- `src/engine/flush/mod.rs` and `src/engine/flush/batcher.rs` — `Batcher` (the payloads that arrived
  together, the order they arrived in, and the one call that writes them) and `Stats` with its
  accessors and the two ways the write path charges it;
- `src/engine/spool/batch.rs` — `Spool::append_batch` and `Spool::stats`, the two signatures this
  step adds to the write path. The file exists for the reason `spool/durable.rs` and
  `spool/recover.rs` do: a stub the learner rewrites *in place* cannot be delivered by an insertion
  (`docs/authoring.md` §5 rule 6);
- `tests/budget.rs` — the course's harness, which is what `slow:bench-budget@10` runs;
- `docs/write-amplification.md` — the page, as a template: the counters, the metrics and the
  baselines are yours to state, and the spec reads the page back.

Every `todo!()` in those files is yours, and so are two things the scaffold cannot hand you: the
accept loop in `src/service/ingest.rs` (write a turn's frames as one batch, and keep the promise
above), and the line the daemon prints at a stop in `src/bin/lakeletd.rs`.

## TRAP

1. **A batch written as N writes.** One open, then a write per record, passes every case that asks
   what the file holds — the bytes are identical — and it fails the counter that exists for it: the
   harness prints `calls_per_record_max` and the gate reports it above its bound, and the daemon's
   `store write cost` line reports as many calls as records.
2. **Answering the batch and writing it later.** A batcher that hands its payloads to a background
   task, a timer or the next turn keeps every read-back case green and breaks `4.1`'s promise. What
   catches it is the moment the read happens: the spec reads `ingest.log` as soon as the daemon says
   `connection closed accepted=8` and finds fewer than eight records.
3. **The header rule lost in the loop.** The log's `LKLT` header travels with the *first* record and
   only with it. A batch whose every record is `segment::encode` is a file that is not a log
   (`Spool::records` refuses it, and recovery proves zero records), and one whose every record is
   `segment::introduce` writes a header into the middle of the log — where the next replay stops for
   good. The cut sweep fails loudly on both.
4. **A batch that reorders, groups or deduplicates.** Sorting payloads before writing them (to make
   the write sequential, say) keeps the byte counts identical and hands every reader a different
   series. `a_batch_keeps_the_order_it_was_given` is the case, and its message names the payload
   that moved.
5. **A counter the caller charges.** Calling `stats.charge(n, bytes, 2)` from the batcher instead of
   at the two syscalls makes the numbers lie with the store still opening per record: the budget
   passes and the write path did not change. Charge where the call is made.
6. **A flush with nothing to write.** An empty batch must write nothing (no open, no counter, no
   batch). An implementation that opens the file anyway spends two calls on a turn in which nothing
   arrived, and `an_empty_batch_writes_nothing_and_costs_nothing` says so — which matters because in
   `4.6` a turn with nothing in it is the common case.
7. **The policy asked per record.** Moving the write into `append_batch` and leaving `self.sync()`
   inside a loop over payloads turns one device call per batch into one per record: the harness's
   `fsyncs_per_batch` is then 64 rather than 1, and a store that waited per record is exactly what
   `4.3` paid to avoid.
8. **Forgetting the policy entirely.** A new write call that never asks `Policy` leaves
   `durable_offset` behind the bytes it wrote under any waiting policy, and `4.3`'s own spec goes red
   — the failure will not look like this step's.
9. **Not counting the device flush.** `syncs` is charged in `Spool::sync`, after the device answered:
   a stop that flushes and a batch that waited both move it, and a `syncs` that stays at zero under
   `per-record` is the sign that the counter was never wired.
10. **A stop line that reads the counters after the stop's own flush.** The daemon prints `store
    write cost` *before* the flush it does on the way out, so `syncs` is what the appends asked for.
    Printing it afterwards reports one device call more than the write path made, and the spec reads
    the line.
11. **A page with the metrics missing.** The spec's last case reads
    `docs/write-amplification.md` and fails on a blank or on a metric it does not name — the page and
    the baseline have to be written in one vocabulary, or a reviewer is left guessing which number
    the gate is guarding.
12. **A duration anywhere in the measurement.** Timestamps in the harness, a `Instant::now()` in a
    counter, a metric that is a millisecond: the budget lane judges counts (handoff M2 §1.1), and a
    harness line that is not `<name>=<value>` is reported as a broken harness rather than compared.
    The same numbers on a loaded machine as on an idle one is the whole reason this lane exists.
13. **A `todo!()` left in any of the four files.** `no-todo` runs over every target, including the
    ones the spec never calls.

## FURTHER

- **Group commit** is what databases do about the same arithmetic: PostgreSQL's `commit_delay` and
  `CommitTsControlLock`, InnoDB's `innodb_flush_log_at_trx_commit`, and MySQL's binlog group commit
  all let one device call serve many transactions, and all of them make a client wait for a batch
  mate. Read one of their implementations with the counters this step measures in mind: the numbers
  they publish are calls per transaction and bytes per commit, never milliseconds per commit.
- **`writev(2)`** hands many buffers to one write call without assembling them first. This project
  builds the batch into one buffer instead — the batch is bounded by the read chunk, so the copy is
  bounded too — and `std::io::IoSlice` is the type that would replace the buffer if it were not.
  **`O_APPEND`** is what makes the batch's single write land at the end without a seek of our own,
  and **`BufWriter`** is the same idea one layer up: fewer, larger writes, at the cost of a copy and
  of bytes a caller believes are written before they are.
- `lakelet/4.6-ingest-backpressure` is where the batch size meets a queue: what this step writes in
  one call is whatever arrived in one turn, and nothing here bounds how much that is.
- `lakelet/4.7-columnar-segments` and `lakelet/4.8-compaction` are steps whose *file layout* changes.
  The counters and the page stay: bytes per sample and calls per record are how those steps argue
  about what their encodings and merges cost.
