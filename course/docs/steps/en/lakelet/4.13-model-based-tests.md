# A model of the store, and a random walk that has to agree with it

## WHY

Twelve steps of cases, and every one of them is a case somebody thought of. That is the shape of a
hand-written suite and it is not a criticism: a case that states a rule is how a rule becomes true.
But the bug that costs you a week is the one nobody thought of, and a store has more of those than
anything you have written so far — a flush that closes a segment in the middle of a merge, a
retention pass whose cutoff lands *inside* a segment, a reopen that finds an index naming a file the
device never kept, a clock that steps backwards between two appends and puts a record behind one it
was appended after. You cannot enumerate those. There are too many of them, and the ones you would
enumerate are the ones you already understand.

The technique that finds them is older than property testing and it is not a library: write a
second, *stupid* implementation of the thing a **reader** of your store sees, drive both with a
random sequence of operations, and compare after every operation. The stupid one is a **reference
model**. It is allowed to be ten lines, it is allowed to be slow, and it is not allowed to know
anything about how the real one keeps its data — because that is the whole trick. A model with a
segment in it would have the same bug as the store.

So what does a reader see? The records that were appended, in the order they were appended, minus
the prefix a retention pass has forgotten. That is it. A flush writes a file, a merge rewrites four
of them into one, a crash leaves an orphan, a reopen replays a log and deletes what the index does
not name — and a reader cannot tell any of it happened. That is not a nice property, it is *the*
property: it is what makes a merge something you can run on a store that is serving queries, and it
is what this step's walk spends sixty-four operations trying to break.

The other half of a property test is the half that makes it useful: when the walk disagrees, it has
to say *what* disagrees, in a sequence short enough to read. A seed and a transcript of sixty-four
operations is a rumour; the same failure with the operations nobody needs deleted from it is a
case. That is **shrinking**, and it is the difference between a red build that gets muted and a red
build that gets fixed.

## TASK

Everything the walk needs is in the tree. Two files are yours, the harness is not — and the split is
deliberate, because the generator and the model are the lesson and a walk that printed a transcript
is a device.

**`src/engine/rng.rs` — yours.** A seeded generator, six methods, and no new dependency: `rand` is a
good crate and the wrong one here, because its own documentation declines to promise what a walk
needs most — that the same seed is the same numbers on every machine, for ever. What is in the file
is the state, the three shifts and the constant the multiply is by; what is decided in the doc
comments is every rule around them, and each of those rules is a decision a case pins:

- **a zero seed is a legal seed.** Zero is the one state xorshift cannot leave — all three shifts of
  it are zero — and the first thing anybody types into `KILN_SIM_SEED` is `0`. Nudge it off zero
  once, where the correction is visible, rather than refusing it;
- **`below(0)` answers `0`.** Drawing from an empty alphabet has one sane answer, and a generator
  that panicked here would fail inside somebody else's process — and it costs nothing: an alphabet
  with no letters in it hands no letter out, so the stream does not move;
- **`between` includes both ends.** A clock, a duration and a timestamp are all "somewhere in this
  span, and either end of it is a legal instant"; a half-open range would make the last millisecond
  of every span unreachable, which is exactly the blind spot a walk exists to find in other code —
  and a span that names nothing answers the boundary without drawing either, which is `below(0)`'s
  rule one level up;
- **`chance(150)` is `always`.** A probability written as a whole number above a hundred is not a
  validated setting, it is an emphatic yes;
- **`pick` panics on an empty alphabet.** It has no value to answer with, and inventing one would be
  a walk playing an operation nobody wrote.

The spec pins a **golden vector**: the first four numbers this generator answers for seed `1`. That
is not a taste test. The walk's whole finding is the sequence, so a change to the arithmetic is a
change to every seed in the project rather than a refactor — and a case that says "these four
numbers" fails loudly instead of quietly moving somebody's bug report.

**`tests/support/model.rs` — yours, and it is meant to be stupid.** A list of the records the store
was told, and one number: the floor. Seven methods, and the only two decisions with any content are
that a read returns what was appended *in that order* (one list, not a map — a reader is handed one
list whatever the records are of) and that expiry moves a floor rather than deleting anything,
because the store does not delete either: a segment that straddles the cutoff keeps its old records
on the disk and stops answering with them.

It lives in `tests/support/` and not in `src/`, because it is a *test's* idea of the store rather
than the store's: a model is a second implementation of a contract, written for a test to disagree
with, and shipping it in the engine would put a second answer to "what does a read return" inside
the thing being judged. This step's spec reads the file as text and fails if it names a segment, a
snapshot, a manifest or a compaction — a model that knew how the store keeps a record would be a
second store, and a walk that compared the two would prove nothing about the first.

**The harness is not yours to write; it is yours to read.** `tests/support/walk.rs` and
`tests/sim_model.rs` arrive complete, and they are the two files to read slowly:

- **a walk is a seed.** One number in, a sequence of operations out — `Append`, `Flush`, `Merge`,
  `Expire`, `Reopen`, `Query` — drawn with *your* generator. Which operation, which series, how many
  records, which way the clock steps and which question gets asked all come out of that seed and
  nothing else: no clock, no path, no address, no hash seed;
- **the clock steps backwards** about a quarter of the time, and never leaves a four-hour window,
  so a walk's whole history is one span of simulated time a pass or a question can name;
- **the comparison happens after every operation**, not at the end. A walk that only compared the
  final state would report "sixty-four operations and the answers differ"; comparing after each one
  names the operation that changed the answer, which is the difference between a failing run and
  something somebody can act on;
- **the transcript is counts, names and digests**, never a path, an address, a wall clock or an
  unmasked float. That is what `slow:sim@3` compares between two runs of the same seed, so it is not
  a logging convenience — it is the contract;
- **a failure is shrunk before it is reported**, so the sequence in the log is one a person reads.

**What "agreed" means.** After every operation,

- the records a reader can see — `snapshot().records()`, the segments the manifest names, with
  everything below the epoch's floor left out — are `answerable()`, record for record, every field
  compared (the value by *bits*, so `-0.0` and `NaN` are the values they are);
- the store's floor is `floor()`, which is the number a retention pass wrote into the manifest;
- and after a `Query`, the buckets are the buckets. The question is asked twice on purpose: once
  through `lakelet/4.11-query-engine` — a plan drawn from the manifest, the segments it keeps, one
  read each — and once by `lakelet/4.10-downsampling` over the model's records. Both sides using
  the same window pass is deliberate: the grid, the partial buckets and the five aggregates are that
  step's subject and its spec's, and a walk that re-derived them would be a second implementation of
  them. What this walk judges is the store's **bookkeeping**: which segments a query opens, what a
  merge replaced, what a reopen replayed, and where the floor is.

**The sharpest edge, and the one rule worth reading twice.** The model is told about a batch when a
**flush closes it into a segment**, not when the append is accepted. A record the log holds and no
segment holds is not an answer: this build's reader is handed segments and a manifest, and
`lakelet/4.11-query-engine` reads exactly that. So an accepted-but-unflushed record is invisible,
and the comparison after an `Append` is the claim that accepting a record changes nothing anybody
can read. A model told at the append fails on the first one — `the store answers 0 record(s) and the
model 2` — and the store is right.

**The seed, and what the course judges with it.** `slow:sim@3` runs this harness once per seed
(`KILN_SIM_SEED=1`, `7` and `20260101`), and judges three things about the runs: each one is green,
the seed marked `replay` prints byte-identical transcripts twice, and at least two seeds print
*different* transcripts — three identical ones would mean the harness never read the seed, so one
walk would have been reported three times. The last one is why an agreeing transcript deliberately
carries no seed: a seed printed beside "agreed" would make three walks differ for a reason that
says nothing about the walk. A failing run is the exception, and there the seed is the first thing a
reader needs.

**The shrinker's contract**, which the spec asserts rather than assumes: the answer still
disagrees; its operations are the ones it was given, in the same order, with some left out; and **no
single operation can be taken out of it and still leave a disagreement**. Deliberately "nothing
obvious left to remove" rather than "the smallest": the smallest is a search over every subset, and
what a person reads in a bug report is the sequence where every remaining operation is load-bearing.

## TRAP

1. **A model that deletes what the floor forgot.** `expire` written as
   `accepted.retain(|record| record.ts_millis >= floor)` looks like the tidier model and it passes
   every walk in the harness — because a read cannot see what it deleted either. It fails the case
   that counts: `len()` is what the store *holds*, the floor forgot records without the store losing
   them, and `model.len() == 32` while `answerable().len() == 20` is the whole of
   `lakelet/4.9-retention` on this side of the comparison.
2. **A floor the model works out for itself.** `expire(cutoff)` takes the cutoff as given — the
   walk hands it the number a pass would write into the manifest, which is `now - keep` snapped
   *down* to a bucket boundary. A model that snaps it again, or that treats the policy window as
   its own, lands on `1700019900000` where the store wrote `1700020500000` and the walk reports the
   floor mismatch before it reports a single record.
3. **`chance` written as `below(percent) == 0`.** It reads like the same thing and it is not:
   `chance(0)` then fires, because `below(0)` is `0` and `0 == 0`. The case that catches it draws a
   thousand times with `percent = 0` and expects nothing; a walk would only catch it as a clock that
   steps backwards far more often than a quarter of the time.
4. **A half-open `between`.** `low + below(high - low)` is the natural first draft, and it never
   draws the last instant of the span — the millisecond a window ends on, the timestamp that is
   exactly the floor. The case asks for every instant of `3..=8` in ten thousand draws; a walk would
   not notice, because a clock that never lands on a boundary still walks.
5. **A generator that is not the pinned one.** Any arithmetic that "looks equivalent" — a different
   multiplier, the shifts in another order, `below` consulting the high bits — is a different
   project: the golden vector fails, and every replay, every bug report and every seed of every
   walk with it. Read the four numbers in the case before changing the constant.
6. **`pick` inventing an element for an empty alphabet.** `items[below(len)]` on an empty slice
   panics with an index message, which is what the case wants; a version that answers
   `items.first().unwrap_or(&default)` silently plays an operation nobody wrote, and a walk would
   report it as a store that agrees with a model of something else.

  contract before reading its body.

  floor).

  `@invariant` split that decides what is checked after every step.

  worth using.

  star come from, and what the multiplier buys.
