# Overload has to be a decision, not an out-of-memory kill

## RECALL

- `async/backpressure@apply — relay/2.14`: the bound is taken *before* the work it is for, and the
  number that grows under load is the one something else can hold — a permit per in-flight attempt
  there, `503` past `max_backlog`, and the row count in the table growing instead of the process.
  This step is the same argument made about the daemon's own memory: the queue lives in the service,
  its capacity is a deployment's number, and what happens at the bound is a named policy instead of
  whatever the allocator decides.
- `io/batching@design — lakelet/4.5-batch-writes`: one write call carries N records, the counters say
  what those calls cost, and the **batch boundary is the frames one turn of the accept loop decoded**.
  That boundary is exactly what this step makes bounded: a turn can deliver more frames than the
  writer can write, and the queue is what stands between the two.
- `async/backpressure@aware — mailer/1.14-worker`: a channel with a capacity and an answer for a
  caller who arrives when it is full. None of that changes here; what changes is that the answer is
  now one of four *decisions*, that each of them has a counter, and that none of them can quietly
  take back something the write path already wrote.

## DELTA

`4.5` left one sentence in its own FURTHER, and it is this step's assignment: what the write path
writes in one call is whatever arrived in one turn, **and nothing bounds how much that is**. A
batcher on a timer or on a size threshold would have written a batch its caller had already answered
for, so `4.5` deliberately did not add one. The shape that resolves the argument is a queue with a
bound in front of the writer, and the price it charges is stated up front: **the producer is made to
wait when there is no room** — the wait `4.5` would have had to invent is the one this step makes
explicit and gives a policy.

Three things are new, and none of them is the queue itself:

- **The daemon had no bound between accepting and writing.** A turn's frames went from the socket
  into a batch in the same breath, so the only limit on how much a connection could make the daemon
  hold was what the socket happened to deliver. Now the queue holds at most `queue.capacity` frames,
  and *nothing a producer does* can make that number grow — the bound is the length of the deque the
  queue is, so there is no second number anywhere that could disagree with it.
- **The overload decision is a value, and every one of them is counted.** `Block`, `DropNewest`,
  `DropOldest` and `Sample` are four answers to the same question ("what now?"), they are visible in
  one enum, and a counter says what each one cost. A deployment that would rather drop frames than
  make its producers wait says so in a configuration key; the default says the opposite, because a
  default must not lose data.
- **The write is still the only acknowledgement.** `4.1` promised that an accepted frame is one the
  client may believe is safe, and `4.5` kept that promise while writing a group of frames; a queue
  between the two makes the promise *harder*, not easier, because the connection no longer writes
  anything itself. So the answer moved: a frame is written when the writer says so, a caller is told
  what happened to each of its own frames by a tally the queue charges, and a connection reports
  nothing until the queue has settled. A frame the queue dropped is never one of the frames a caller
  was told it stored — and that is now arithmetic a spec can check rather than a sentence in a
  document.

**Why the capacity is a deployment's key and `4.3`'s fsync policy was not.** The two look alike and
are not: when an append waits for the device is a property of what this store *is* (a WAL kept by an
interval policy, decided once in an ADR and shipped in the build), while how much a daemon may hold
between the socket and the log is a property of where it is *run* — the same binary serves a laptop
and a machine with 64 GiB, and a frame's payload may be up to a megabyte, so `queue.capacity = 1024`
is "a few hundred kibibytes of ordinary telemetry" or "a gigabyte of the largest frames the protocol
allows" depending on the workload. A number whose meaning depends on the deployment is the
deployment's to choose; that is the rule, and this step is where it is applied.

What is deliberately **not** here, so a reader knows where the next step starts: the fsync decision
(`4.3`), what opening a store recovers (`4.4`), the record format and the write-cost counters (`4.5`,
untouched), and segments and compaction (`4.7`, `4.8`) — where the *same* question comes back with
different nouns: merging segments that do not both fit is "who wins when two things need one slot",
and the answer there will be a policy too.

## TASK

Five properties make up this step. Everything else is packaging.

- **Bounded, always.** The queue holds at most its capacity in frames, for every policy, under a
  producer that never stops. The bound is a deployment's number in the layered configuration
  (`queue.capacity`, with `queue.policy` beside it), it has a default, and a deployment that asks for
  a queue of zero frames is refused with a sentence naming the key — a daemon that says it accepts
  nothing and then starts is worse than one that does not start.
- **A drop is a decision, and every one of them is counted.** The four policies, exactly:
  - `Block` waits for room and then takes the frame in: nothing is dropped, and the producer is what
    feels the overload;
  - `DropNewest` refuses the *arriving* frame — the frame that pays is the frame that just arrived,
    and it comes back to the caller so the line the daemon logs can name it;
  - `DropOldest` always takes the arrival and drops the frame that has been **waiting longest**: the
    loss lands on data that arrived earlier, and the queue's depth does not move while it happens;
  - `Sample` takes **one arrival in `SAMPLE_KEEP`** and refuses the rest — it is the one policy that
    drops frames without the queue being full, because it is a *rate* rather than a bound, and what
    it protects is the write path's throughput instead of the memory. A kept arrival that finds no
    room makes room the way `DropOldest` does.
  A counter says how many frames the queue dropped; the *decision* says which frame, and the
  overload is reported: a log line per dropped frame and the daemon's own tally at a stop.
- **The acknowledgement never moves ahead of the write, and nothing dropped is ever acknowledged.**
  That is `4.1`'s promise, kept through a third party: a frame is written when the write path says
  so (`wrote`, called *after* the bytes are on the file, never when the frames were handed over), a
  caller's tally is charged at that moment, and `DropOldest` may only take back a frame that is still
  **queued** — never one the writer has taken, and never one that is already written. A connection
  waits for the queue to settle before it reports what it accepted.
- **`offered == accepted + dropped`, and `accepted == written + queued`.** State both in the page and
  in the code, and enforce them per policy, at every checkpoint. They are one statement: `accepted`
  counts the frames the queue took in and has not dropped again, so a frame an eviction takes back
  leaves `accepted` with it — an implementation that lets it stay counted is an implementation whose
  two equations disagree, and the case that catches it is the one that offers more than the bound
  under `DropOldest` and `Sample`.
- **Under overload, the schedule decides who is dropped** — which is why this step is judged by
  `slow:sim@3` and not by a single run. Three seeds, one transcript per seed, the same seed replaying
  byte for byte, and at least two seeds differing: the harness prints the seed nowhere, so the
  digests differ because the events differ.

**What the course gives you, and what is yours.** The scaffold declares the surface, because three
things read it — the spec, the harness, and the daemon:

- `src/service/backpressure.rs` — `Policy` (with `as_str`/`parse`, so the configuration key and the
  log lines spell the four decisions the same way), `Decision` (what happened to one frame),
  `Tally` (what one caller is told), `Stats` (the four counters), and `Queue` with its method
  signatures and `todo!()` bodies. The **state behind `Queue` is yours**: this file deliberately
  declares no fields, because where the bound lives is the first design decision of the step. The
  bound is the queue's own length and a second number that tracks the same thing is a second number
  that can disagree with it;
- `tests/sim_backpressure.rs` — the course's harness, which is what `slow:sim@3` runs: four phases,
  one per policy, three producers racing into a bound of three, and the conservation law asserted
  *inside* the scenario per policy;
- `src/service/mod.rs` and `src/service/config.rs` — the module declaration and the two `Config`
  fields, inserted into the marked regions the skeleton declares. Every `todo!()` in the contract file
  is yours, and so are the parts a scaffold cannot reach: the four layers' wiring for the two keys
  (default, file, environment, flag — `4.1`'s ladder, unchanged), the accept loop that offers a turn's
  frames, the writer task that drains the queue, and the two lines the daemon prints.

Two layers of counters, and they answer different questions: the **queue's** `Stats` is what the
daemon saw and did with it, and a **caller's `Tally`** is what one connection may say about its own
frames. Both are needed, because a shared bound can drop a frame that was offered by somebody who has
already gone away — and that is exactly the case a per-connection line cannot guess at.

**The two keys, spelled out.** The names are the contract — the configuration file, the environment,
the command line and the report all reach the same two settings, and the spec drives every one of
them:

| layer | the capacity | the policy |
| --- | --- | --- |
| the default | `DEFAULT_CAPACITY` — 1024 frames | `DEFAULT_POLICY` — `block` |
| `lakelet.toml` | `[queue]` `capacity = 8` | `[queue]` `policy = "drop-oldest"` |
| the environment | `LAKELET_QUEUE_CAPACITY=16` | `LAKELET_QUEUE_POLICY=sample` |
| the command line | `--queue-capacity 32` | `--queue-policy drop-newest` |
| what a deployment reads back | `queue.capacity = 32` | `queue.policy = drop-newest` |

A value no layer can use is a refusal from the layer that carried it (`4.1`'s rule: an unrecognised
key must not quietly become a default), and the two that matter here are a capacity of **zero frames**
— a queue that can never take a frame in is a refusal naming its key, not a daemon that starts — and a
policy name nobody speaks, which is refused with a sentence listing the four that this build has.

The daemon prints two things an operator reads: one line per frame the queue dropped, saying
`frame dropped by the queue` with `policy=<name>` and `bytes=<the payload's length>`, and one at a
stop saying `queue drained` with `policy`, `capacity`, `offered`, `accepted`, `dropped` and `written`.
Nothing in either is a duration.

## TRAP

1. **A bound kept somewhere other than the frames.** A counter incremented beside the queue, a
   semaphore whose permits are not the deque's slots, a `Vec` whose `len()` is checked after the
   push: each is a second number that can disagree with what the queue actually holds, and the case
   that catches it is the producer that never stops (a thousand frames offered against a bound of
   three, with the depth read after every one of them).
2. **A producer that wakes itself.** `Block` has to park until there is room, so it has to tell the
   writer about the frames it already handed over — and if it signals the same object it is about to
   await, it completes its own future (`tokio`'s `Notify` promises it will, for the
   create-before-check idiom) and spins in a loop that makes no progress. The symptom is a
   `block_waits_for_room_instead_of_dropping` that never finishes while the CPU is at 100%, which is
   a hang you have to recognise rather than a red test you have to read.
3. **Acknowledging at offer time.** Counting a frame as accepted when the queue takes it in is the
   defect this step exists to prevent, and it is invisible until the write is late: the frame is in
   the queue, the connection has already printed its line, and `4.1`'s burst case finds fewer records
   than the line claims. Under `DropOldest` it is worse than late — the frame can be evicted after
   the line that called it stored, so the daemon reports data it never wrote.
4. **Counting an evicted frame as accepted.** Taking the frame back out of the deque without letting
   `accepted` go with it makes `offered == accepted + dropped` fail by exactly the number of
   evictions: twelve frames into a bound of one under `Sample` read as `offered=12 accepted=3
   dropped=11`, and the case says which policy it was.
5. **`DropNewest` that evicts, or `DropOldest` that refuses.** The two policies are distinguished by
   *which* frame is lost, not by how many: `DropNewest` refuses the arriving frame (and the payload
   that comes back is the one just offered), `DropOldest` takes the arrival in and gives back the
   frame that waited longest. A case per policy checks the identities, so an implementation that
   drops the right *number* of frames is still red.
6. **`Sample` that only samples when full.** Then it is `DropNewest` with extra steps: the arrivals
   are not counted, the 4th, 8th and 12th are not the ones kept, and the case that offers twelve
   frames under `SAMPLE_KEEP = 4` fails on which ones survived.
7. **A `Block` that refuses instead of waiting.** The cheapest way to make a test stop hanging is to
   answer `Refused` when there is no room — and it turns the one policy that loses nothing into one
   that loses whatever arrives at the wrong moment. `dropped == 0` is the case, and it is checked at
   a checkpoint, not only at the end.
8. **A capacity of zero that starts anyway.** `queue.capacity = 0` must be a refusal naming the key,
   from every layer that can carry it; a daemon that starts with a queue that can never take a frame
   in is a daemon whose only behaviour is to drop everything, and a deployment finds that out in
   production.
9. **A batch per frame.** Offering each frame of a turn on its own — or a writer that takes one frame
   at a time — keeps every behavioural case green and moves the counters the wrong way:
   `4.5`'s daemon case reports as many calls as records, and the budget's `calls_per_record_max`
   fails. The turn is one offer and the writer takes everything waiting, which is what makes the
   batch size the queue's decision.
10. **A writer that reports the write before making it.** `wrote` is called after the bytes are on
    the file and not when the frames were taken; the queue's own `written` counter and every caller's
    tally move there, and the case that watches the window between `take` and `wrote` asserts that
    nothing has been written yet.
11. **A seed in the transcript.** Printing `seed=7` in a `lakelet-sim` line makes two seeds differ
    without any event having differed, so the "at least two transcripts differ" half of
    `slow:sim@3` stops meaning anything. What tells the seeds apart here is the order of the events.
12. **A transcript with a clock or an address in it.** Formatting a `tokio::time::Instant` puts the
    machine's real monotonic base into the transcript, and the same seed then produces two different
    digests — `the same seed produced two different transcripts`, which is the failure
    `docs/handoff-m3-log.md` §6 measured. Print what the scenario *observed*, and nothing else.
13. **`tokio::net::*` in the harness.** The real socket types need `enable_tokio_io()`, and from
    there the addresses become real: a simulated address answers `AddrNotAvailable` and a shared port
    `AddrInUse`. Bind `turmoil::net::TcpListener` and connect by host name, which is what the
    simulated DNS resolves.
14. **A `todo!()` left in any of the three files.** `no-todo` runs over every target, including the
    ones the spec never calls — and the scaffold state is *supposed* to be red, so the first
    `cargo test -p lakelet --test spec_backpressure` failing every case is this step's starting line
    rather than a broken repository.

## FURTHER

- **`lakelet/4.8-compaction`** is where the same question comes back in the storage layer: merging
  segments when they do not both fit is another "who wins when two things need one slot", and the
  answer will be another policy with another counter. Read this step's conservation law against it:
  the shape ("everything that came in is written, dropped, or still in hand") is the reusable part.
- **`tokio::sync::mpsc`** is the ecosystem's version of this file: `send` waits for capacity and
  `try_send` refuses, which is `Block` and `DropNewest` in one type. What it does *not* have is
  `DropOldest` or `Sample`, and the reason is worth working out — the receiver owns the ordering, so
  a sender cannot see the frame that has been waiting longest. `tokio_util::sync::CancellationToken`
  is the other half of a stop path that drains instead of dropping.
- **`tokio::sync::Notify`**'s documentation, especially the sentence about a future created *before*
  the condition is checked: that guarantee is what makes the create-before-check idiom correct, and
  TRAP 2 is what it costs when the same object is used on both sides. `tokio::time::Instant` under a
  paused clock (`tokio::time::pause`) is the other tool a deterministic scenario reaches for.
- **OpenTelemetry's sampler** (`TraceIdRatioBased`, `ParentBased`, and the SDK's `BatchSpanProcessor`
  queue with `max_queue_size` and `max_export_batch_size`) is `Sample` and a bound in one
  specification: the sampler thins the stream, the processor's queue bounds the memory, and the
  dropped-span count is the number both report. **Vector's** buffer documentation is the same story
  with `buffer.max_events` and `when_full: block | drop_newest`, and **librdkafka's**
  `queue.buffering.max.messages` with `queue.buffering.max.ms` is the producer-side version a message
  broker ships.
- **The kernel already runs this file.** A socket's receive buffer (`SO_RCVBUF`), the TCP window and
  the listen backlog are the same bound with the same policies — and the kernel's answer to a full
  receive buffer is to stop acknowledging, which is `Block` performed on the sender's behalf.
  `drop_newest` at that layer means silently losing packets, and it is why a protocol that cannot
  afford to lose a frame has to bound its own memory instead: the alternative is a bound nobody
  chose.
