# Cardinality is the bill: cap the series, and price the ones you keep

## WHY

Every number this track has measured so far is a *volume*: bytes per payload byte, blocks a plan did
not read, work per query phase. Volume is the axis an operator can watch. Cardinality is the one they
cannot: a label whose values the *application* chooses — `host=<pod name>`, `user=<id>`,
`request_id=<uuid>` — turns one metric into a million series, and nothing about the bytes going past
tells you that it happened. Each record is still small, each frame is still valid, each write still
succeeds. What changes is the shape of everything downstream of the write path: a million dictionary
entries in the segment format, a million terms in the sidecar index's vocabulary, a million names in
the identity of every answer, and a query planner that now has a million things to group by. A store
dies of cardinality by being *asked* to hold it, not by being slow at holding it.

So the defence is a policy decided before the incident: a cap on how many series this store tracks,
a counter that says when it refused one, and a page that prices the ones it kept. What makes this
step more than a number is *where the cap has to live*. A cap that each process enforces from what
**it** admitted is a cap that resets on every restart — a deployment whose process manager restarts
it hourly grows by a limit every hour, with no counter anywhere saying so. The rule this step lands
on is `lakelet/4.14-index-pruning`'s, applied to cardinality: the ledger of series is **derived
state**, rebuilt from what the store itself holds, so the number a cap is applied to is a fact about
the store and not about the process asking.

## RECALL

`store/columnar@apply` — `lakelet/4.7`: a segment lays its records out in columns, one of which is
the series name, and the name is written once per distinct series rather than once per record. That
is exactly why the bill follows cardinality and not volume, and it is the reason the format's
dictionary is where the first cost of a new name lands.

`obs/metrics-slo@design` — `jobq/3.17`: a metric's identity is the labels somebody chose to group it
by, and every choice that looks free in a dashboard is a multiplication here. The same decision shows
up as an SLO's grouping one track earlier and as a store's series count in this one.

## DELTA

The cap is a policy about **state**, not about traffic. A client sending a million samples of one
series is a client this store is delighted to serve; a client sending one sample each of a million
series is the one that costs money, and a cap on requests would refuse the wrong one of the two.
That is the whole reason the ledger counts *names*.

Two consequences make this step's code different from the counters the last three steps added. First,
the ledger cannot be a file this module writes: a store that is wrong about its own cardinality
because a sidecar went stale is the failure 4.14 argued against, and the answer is the same — rebuild
it from the store (the log's records, pruned by the segments' sidecar indexes) and let a missing
index cost precision instead of an answer. Second, the refusal is not something the client can be
told: the ingest protocol is one-way, and `lakelet/4.1-ingest-protocol` decided that a client learns
what happened to its frames from the counters its *connection* leaves behind and from what the store
can be asked later. `lakeletq inspect series` is that second half.

## TASK

The scaffold created two modules and declared them where the engine's module map keeps them:

- `src/engine/cardinality/cap.rs` — the policy as a value: `Cap` (the limit, `Cap::DEFAULT`,
  `Cap::new`, `limit`) and `Admission` (`Admitted` / `Rejected { tracked, limit }`), with a `Display`
  a log line or a client can read;
- `src/engine/cardinality/mod.rs` — the ledger: `Cost` (records, bytes, first and last millisecond),
  and `Registry` with `admit`, `charge`, `cost_of`, `tracked`, `rebuild`, the three counters
  (`series_admitted`, `series_rejected`, `tracked_count`) and the identity they obey
  (`offered == admitted + rejected`).

It also left `docs/cardinality.md` as a skeleton. The bodies, the wiring and the page are yours.

**The cap's rule, in two sentences.** A series the store already tracks is always admitted — a cap
must not break a series that is being reported, or a deployment at its limit loses the data it
already holds. A series the store has never seen is admitted while the ledger has room and refused
once it does not. The refusal charges nothing, moves nothing, and is counted; and a **frame** is
judged as the batch it is, because the write path stores a payload whole or not at all
(`lakelet/4.5-batch-writes`) — a frame the cap refuses is a frame whose every series was refused,
including the ones the store already has.

**The ledger, rebuilt rather than kept.** `Registry::of_store` is the rule: the records the store's
log carries (the write path never truncates it, `docs/soak.md`) charged to the series they name, then
pruned by the sidecar indexes of the segments the store still holds. A series some live index may
hold is one the store still has; a series no index may hold is one it has forgotten — retention
expired the segments that carried it — and that is what keeps a cap from being a lifetime quota on a
store whose window deliberately forgets. An index that is **missing or damaged** is
`SegmentIndex::unstated`, which may hold everything: it proves nothing, drops nothing, and costs
tracking precision and never an answer. Opening a store is what runs this, which is why a restarted
daemon does not start its count from zero.

**The daemon's half, which is where the refusal becomes visible.** The cap is a deployment's number,
`series.limit`, read through the four layers `lakelet/4.6-ingest-backpressure` established (file <
environment < flag), and `lakeletd` opens the store with it. The ingest path asks the cap *before*
the queue — the queue's answer is the promise that the bytes are on the file, so a frame the store
will not take must never reach the writer — counts the frame in the connection's own `rejected`
(which is what the connection's closing line reports), and logs one line naming the series and the
two numbers the decision was made from. The stop path prints the run's ledger beside the queue's
numbers.

**The tool's half.** `lakeletq inspect series --store <DIR>` prints the ledger the store rebuilds: one
line per tracked series — the name, its records, its bytes, and the two ends of its window — in name
order, and the same objects as one JSON array with `--json`. It is a read: it does not create a
store, and a store with no series is `[]` rather than an error.

**Then the page.** `docs/cardinality.md` is where the numbers live: the cap this build ships and why,
the three counters and the identity, what a refused client can observe, the arithmetic — what a
series costs a day *on the workload you measure*, not on one you assert — and one paragraph on what a
cap costs, because the series you refuse are the ones nobody can query later.

  a thing a long run finds.
- `lakeletq inspect series --store <a store you wrote>` prints what the store holds, and
  `docs/cardinality.md` quotes a run of yours.

## TRAP

1. **A ledger built from what this process admitted.** It passes every case in one process and resets
   on restart — the leak this step exists to prevent, and the reason `Registry::of_store` is called
   at open rather than the counters starting at zero.
2. **Refusing a series the store already tracks.** A deployment that reached its limit then loses the
   series it is already holding, one sample at a time, with no error anywhere. The check is on *new*
   names only.
3. **Admitting half a frame.** The queue's promise is that accepted bytes are written
   (`lakelet/4.5-batch-writes`), so a frame whose tracked series went in and whose new one did not is
   a partial write nothing in the protocol can express.
4. **Reading a clock in the engine.** `Instant::now()` in the ledger makes two runs of one workload
   report two different windows, and a budget or a soak then disagrees with itself. `now` is a
   parameter, and the case that writes it down scans the source for it.
5. **Counting records, or counting a refused frame's series as admitted.** The cap is about names;
   `offered == admitted + rejected` is the line a leak crosses, and it holds per series offered.
6. **Writing the ledger to a file of its own.** That is the second source of truth 4.14 refused: a
   store that is wrong about its own cardinality because a sidecar went stale answers questions about
   a store that is not there.
7. **Reading a bloom's `yes` as a proof.** A `may_hold` that answers `true` costs a read; treating it
   as "the store holds this" turns the prune into a source of wrong answers, and an `unstated` index
   — a segment whose vocabulary nobody could read — may hold everything.
