# Poison pills do not block the queue: give up, isolate, replay

## WHY

A queue that only drains when everything works is a queue that stops at the first subscriber that
does not. Two kinds of delivery end that way, and they are different failures on the same row: a
subscriber that answers `410 Gone` (or `400`, or a target this service is not allowed to talk to)
will never take this event, no matter how many times it is asked; and a subscriber that is *trying*
and cannot manage it will burn the whole attempt budget without getting anywhere. Both are terminal,
and `relay/2.6-at-least-once` already writes them down: the row ends in `dead`.

A row in `dead` that nobody can see is barely better than a retry loop. The queue is moving, which
is the operator's dashboard going green — and the events that did not arrive are invisible: no
listing, no reason, no way to ask for them again. "Give up" without "here is what we gave up on"
is how a delivery service loses an invoice on a Tuesday and learns about it in a month.

So this step delivers the other three quarters:

- **the terminal states are queryable.** One route per tenant, showing every delivery this service
gave up on, where it was going, what the subscriber said, how many attempts it cost, and when;
- **the failure is isolated.** A poison delivery costs *one* attempt and then it is out of the
  queue, so the subscribers that work are not delayed by the one that does not. This is the queue
  version of `relay/2.9-ordering`'s promise, one layer up;
- **there is a way back.** A subscriber that was down for an hour comes back, and the deliveries
  that died in that hour are the ones its users are missing. `POST /dlq/{id}/replay` puts one back
  in the queue: the same delivery (the receiver deduplicates on the id), a fresh budget, and the
  history of the life that just ended kept where a person can read it.

The interesting part is the *boundary* of that last one, which is why it takes half the step: a
replay resurrects a dead letter and nothing else. A delivery a subscriber has taken is not
resurrected (sending it again is a duplicate nobody asked for), a delivery that is already owed
again is not resurrected twice, and a delivery that is not yours does not exist.

## TASK

The scaffold added `migrations/0006_dead_letters.sql` (one row per life a delivery lost, and the
partial index the listing reads) and `src/dlq.rs` — `DeadLetter`, `Life`, `Replay`, `list` and
`replay` with `todo!()` bodies. No new dependency. The routes and the contract document are yours.

### `src/lib.rs`

Declare the module: `pub mod dlq;`.

### `src/dlq.rs`

- **`list(pool, tenant, limit)`** — one tenant's dead letters, newest death first, each with its
  whole history: `lives`, oldest first, `replayed_at` set on the lives a replay has undone. The
  listing is per tenant because a dead letter names an endpoint and an event, and those belong to
  one tenant: filter on the *event's* tenant, not on the endpoint's, or a deleted endpoint loses its
  dead letters.
- **`replay(pool, tenant, delivery) -> Option<Replay>`** — three writes that have to agree, so one
  transaction: read the delivery *with `for update`*, decide, then move the row (`pending`, no
  attempts, due now) and stamp the open history rows as replayed. `None` is "no such delivery for
  this tenant", which is also the answer for somebody else's — a caller must not be able to tell
  the two apart, and that is why the route answers `404` and not `403`.
- **`Replay::NotDead`** — the boundary: a delivery that is owed again, or one that was delivered, is
  not a dead letter, and a replay that reset it anyway would send a duplicate. Answer `200` with
  `replayed: false` and the state it is in. It is not an error to ask, and a caller that retries a
  replay (because its own request timed out) has to get the same answer as the first call.
- **What a replay does *not* do**: it does not change the delivery's id (the receiver's
deduplication key), it does not delete the history, and it does not clear `last_error` — the row
  keeps the reason it died until a delivery succeeds (`mark_sent` is what clears it).

### `src/claim.rs`: `mark_dead` writes the letter

The state and the history must not be able to disagree: `mark_dead` becomes one statement that moves
the row to `dead` and inserts the `dead_letters` row in the same breath. The `where state =
'claimed'` guard has to survive that refactor — a row that is not this worker's writes no letter —
and the answer is the number of letters written, so its signature and its meaning do not change.

### The routes (`src/http.rs`)

- `GET /dlq` — the calling tenant's dead letters, `?limit=` bounded (default 50, most 200, anything
  else a `400`). Behind the same authentication layer as `/events`: a listing of failures is not
  public.
- `POST /dlq/{id}/replay` — `200` with `{id, replayed, replays, state}` (the same four fields
  whether it replayed or not, so a client parses one shape), `400` for something that is not a
  delivery id, `404` for a delivery this tenant cannot see.

### `src/openapi.rs`

Both routes go into `document()`, with every status they can answer and a schema for each body —
including the `404` and the `400`, because "every answer a caller can get has to be in the contract"
is what `relay/2.3-contract-first`'s spec checks. The schemas are closed (`additionalProperties: false`) and name their `required` fields.

  not touch the other subscribers; the listing names what died and where and why; a budget that ran
  out is a dead letter too; a replay is a new life with the old one kept; a replay does nothing to a
  delivery that is not dead; another tenant can neither see nor replay one

  with their histories, and `curl -X POST .../dlq/<id>/replay` moves one out of it

## TRAP

1. **A dead letter nobody can read.** The row is in `dead`, the queue is clean, and the event is
   gone with no listing and no reason. `attempts` and `last_error` were already there since
   `relay/2.6`; this step is what makes them answerable.
2. **One listing for all tenants.** `GET /dlq` without a tenant filter is a cross-tenant leak with
   an audit trail attached: every dead letter names an event id, a `kind` and another tenant's
   endpoint URL. Filter on the event's tenant (the event is the tenant's; the endpoint may have been
   deleted, and its dead letters are still the tenant's business).
3. **A replay that copies the delivery.** Inserting a *new* row with a new id is the tempting shape,
   and it breaks the receiver's only deduplication key: the subscriber sees a second event it cannot
   tell from a real one. The id does not change; the row is resurrected.
4. **A replay that keeps the attempts.** Resetting the state but not `attempts` gives the delivery
   a budget it has already spent: it is claimed, attempted, and either dies on the first failure or
   burns a count nobody can explain. Attempts back to zero, and the old count kept in the history.
5. **A replay that erases the history.** Overwriting the row's `last_error` (or deleting the letters)
   destroys the answer to "has this happened before?", which is the first question an operator asks
   after the second replay. Every life is a row; a replay only stamps `replayed_at`.
6. **A replay with no boundary.** Resurrecting anything the caller names — a `sent` delivery, one
   that is already `pending` — turns a bug-fixing tool into a duplicate-generator, and two replays
   of one id into two lives of it. Only `dead` moves, and the answer says what happened instead.
7. **Two writes, no transaction.** The row moves and the history is stamped in two statements, and a
   crash in between leaves a delivery owed again with a history that says nobody replayed it — and
   two concurrent replays of one id can both see `dead` and both resurrect it. One transaction, and
   read the row `for update`.
8. **`403` for another tenant's delivery.** It is the honest status and the wrong answer: it tells
   a caller that the id exists. `404` for both "gone" and "not yours" is the same rule
   `mailer/1.18-authz-boundary` used.
9. **A `400` for a delivery that is not dead.** Replaying a delivered row is a caller mistake — or a
   client retrying its own request. An error makes the retry impossible to distinguish from the
   mistake; `200` with `replayed: false` makes both of them a read.
10. **An unbounded listing.** `GET /dlq` that answers with everything a tenant has ever lost is a
    query that gets slower exactly when it matters, and a response an operator cannot read. Bound it,
    document the bound, and say in the body that a very large DLQ wants the cursor of
    `relay/2.4-cursor-pagination`.
11. **A route that is not in the document.** A route a client cannot find in `/openapi.json` is a
    route that only this service knows about, and `relay/2.3-contract-first`'s spec fails the whole
    step when the code and the document disagree — including the statuses you added and forgot to
    describe.
