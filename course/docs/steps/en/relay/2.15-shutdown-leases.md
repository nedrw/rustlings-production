# Shutdown with in-flight leases: recovery without a stuck row

## RECALL

`async/graceful-shutdown@apply` — `mailer/1.14-worker`: a process that is asked to stop stops
*taking* work, finishes the work already taken, and exits inside a deadline, because whoever asked
politely also holds a `SIGKILL` for the process that will not stop. There the work was a message in
an in-memory queue, and the deadline was the difference between losing it and not.

## DELTA

The sequence is the same and the price of getting it wrong has changed shape. This service does not
hold its work in memory: a delivery in flight is a row in `claimed`, with the id of the worker
holding it and an instant after which the row is presumed abandoned. That changes three things, and
each one is a decision this step has to make:

- **dropping a task no longer loses the delivery.** A cancelled attempt leaves the row `claimed` by
  a process that no longer exists, so the delivery is not lost — it becomes **invisible for the
  length of the lease**. The deadline you pick is therefore not "how long may the drain take": it is
  "how long am I willing to have this delivery, and every other row this worker held, be handed to
  nobody", and the clock that decides who wins is not yours. A worker that comes back inside the
  lease is a duplicate; one that comes back after it is a second delivery to a subscriber that was
  never told about the first;
- **`kill -9` cannot be handled at all, and this is where that stops being a footnote.** There is no
  handler for it, no log line, and no chance to give anything back. The only thing that recovers
  those rows is the instant written on them, so somebody has to *read* it: a worker that never looks
  at expired leases is a worker that leaves them invisible for ever. That is `reclaim_expired`, and
  it is what makes a crash a delay rather than a loss;
- **a clean stop does not have to wait for that instant.** A worker that is stopping knows it is
  stopping, and it knows which rows it holds — so it gives them back now, and a rollout costs seconds
  rather than a lease. The difference between a deploy that looks like an outage and one nobody
  notices is this one statement.

The consequence worth saying out loud: **two numbers, not one**. `delivery.drain_seconds` decides how
much work this process abandons when it stops, and `delivery.lease_seconds` decides how long the
queue hides the damage from a process that did not stop on purpose. A lease shorter than the slowest
attempt a deployment waits for (`Policy::deadline`, `relay/2.7`) is a lease that expires underneath
the worker holding it, and the same delivery is then attempted by two workers at once — the bound is
on *claims*, and nothing in the row says "this attempt is still alive".

## TASK

The scaffold added `src/lease.rs`: three signatures with `todo!()` bodies. Everything else in this
step is in files you already own, and the shape of what goes where is the step: the queue's rows are
`src/claim.rs`'s (`relay/2.5`), the worker that runs is `src/concurrency.rs`'s (`relay/2.14`), and
what a *stop* means is the new module's.

### `migrations/0007_lease_reclaim.sql`

Recovering expired leases is one `update` over the rows that are `claimed` *and* expired, run once
per worker per turn. Add a partial index in exactly that shape — `on deliveries (lease_expires_at)
where state = 'claimed'` — because without it the statement walks every delivery the service has ever
owed, and the cost of recovering from a crash grows with the tenant's history. The next free
migration number is `0007` (`ls migrations`).

### `src/lease.rs`

- `reclaim_expired(pool)` — every row whose lease has run out goes back to `pending`, due now, with
  no holder, no lease, **and its attempt counted**. Nobody observed what happened to that attempt:
  the subscriber may have been told and may not. Not counting it is how a delivery whose worker keeps
  dying is retried for ever, because the failure count is the only thing that ends a delivery — and
  `relay/2.10-dlq` is where those belong, which means they have to be counted to get there.
- `release_held(pool, worker)` — every row this worker still holds goes back to `pending`, keeping
  its failure count and its instant (nothing about it failed). The filter is `locked_by = $1` and
  that filter is the whole safety property: a worker that could release anybody's lease could hand
  another worker's in-flight attempt to a third one.
- `serve(worker, drain, shutdown)` — the sequence, in this order:
  1. **the signal ends the claiming.** The turn that is running is not cancelled *by* the signal —
     the loop selects on the signal *and* the turn, and a signal that wins drops the turn's future
     (its tasks keep running; see TRAP 1) — and no new turn starts;
  2. **the attempts in flight get `drain`.** `worker.gate().quiesce(drain)` waits for the seats to
     come back, which is exactly "is anything still in flight", and an attempt that answers inside the
     deadline has its delivery recorded as delivered — which is the one thing this sequence is for;
  3. **whatever is left is given back**: `release_held`, so a stop costs seconds rather than a lease;
  4. **an idle queue is not asked again immediately**: a turn that claimed nothing is followed by a
     short wait, or a worker with nothing to do spends a core asking.
  Between turns, `reclaim_expired` — one statement that writes nothing when there is nothing to
  reclaim — so a worker that starts after a crash picks up what the crashed one left.

### `src/concurrency.rs`

The worker's two new pieces: a `lease` field (defaulting to `claim::LEASE`, with `Worker::leasing` to
change it — the claim duration was `relay/2.5`'s constant and it becomes this deployment's decision),
and `Worker::pool()`, because the leas s this worker gives back are rows in *its* database. `Report`'s
two builders become `pub(crate)` so `serve` can count the turns it ran.

### `src/config.rs`, `src/main.rs`

- `delivery.lease_seconds` (default 60) and `delivery.drain_seconds` (default 10), both refused when
  zero, both reported by `--check-config`.
- `main.rs` runs **one worker** over the same pool the HTTP state uses, `HttpSender::signing` over
  `Policy::of(&config)` and `DbSecrets`, named after the run id (the name is what a claim is stamped
  with, and two processes sharing one name would release each other's rows). The signal stops both
  halves, and **both halves are awaited**: `select!` on its own returns as soon as the first one
  finishes and drops the other one's future, which is how an in-flight delivery vanishes with no line
  in the log — the trap `mailer/1.14-worker` names.

## TRAP

1. **`select!` on the signal and dropping the other branch.** The turn that is running when the
   signal arrives has spawned tasks; dropping its future *detaches* them (a `JoinHandle` that is
   dropped does not cancel anything), so they keep running while the shutdown path decides the rows
   are free — and the row is delivered by a task the worker has already stopped accounting for. The
   attempt has to be *waited for* (`quiesce`) or its row handed back, and the difference between those
   two is the drain deadline. The same mistake in `main.rs` ends the process while the drain is still
   running, so `release_held` never happens and the lease is the only recovery left.
2. **A worker that never reads expired leases.** Then `kill -9` is a permanent stuck row: nothing
   writes it down, and no other worker will look at it — the queue simply stops including that
   delivery, and the subscriber never learns. `reclaim_expired` between turns is two lines and it is
   the whole crash story.
3. **Waiting for the lease instead of releasing it.** `serve` that drains and exits without
   `release_held` is correct *and* slow: every rollout makes the deliveries that were in flight
   invisible for a full lease. "The work comes back eventually" is not the same promise as "the work
   comes back when I stop".
4. **Releasing rows you do not hold.** `release_held` without the `locked_by = $1` filter puts *every*
   claimed row back in the queue — including another worker's in-flight attempts, which are then
   claimed and attempted a second time by somebody else. Two attempts, one delivery, nobody to blame.
5. **Forgetting that the drain cuts attempts, not deliveries.** A row whose attempt the deadline cut
   keeps its failure count, because nothing about it failed — and the delivery is attempted again,
   which may deliver a duplicate. Counting it as a failure would make a rollout look like a
   subscriber problem; not counting it is fine *because* the receiver deduplicates on the delivery id
   (`relay/2.11-hmac-signing` signs that id, `relay/2.6-at-least-once` promises it).
6. **A lease shorter than the attempt deadline.** `lease_seconds = 1` with `Policy::deadline = 30s`
   means the worker's own rows expire while it is still waiting for an answer: the queue hands the
   same delivery to another worker, and now two workers are talking to one subscriber. The lease has
   to be longer than the slowest attempt a deployment is willing to wait for, and the two numbers
   live in different sections of the configuration, which is exactly how they get set independently.
7. **A worker name shared by two processes.** `locked_by = 'relay'` for the whole fleet means a
   stopping worker releases rows that another live worker is attempting right now. The name comes
   from the run id, and the reason is the same one as the filter in TRAP 4.
8. **Claiming while draining.** A `serve` that keeps turning while it waits for the drain is a worker
   that takes on work it will not finish — the opposite of the promise `mailer/1.14-worker` made with
   a bounded queue. The signal ends the *loop*, not just the turn.
9. **An idle worker that spins.** A loop of `claim → nothing → claim` is a process that spends a core
   (and a database round trip) proving the queue is empty, once per worker, in every replica. The wait
   after an empty turn is what makes an idle service cheap, and it has to be interruptible by the
   signal — otherwise `SIGTERM` waits out the idle interval.
10. **Trusting the report instead of the rows.** The turn that is running when the signal arrives is
    dropped, so its counts never reach the `Report` that `serve` returns: the deliveries it finished
    are in the database, and that is where they matter. An operator reading "0 sent" in the shutdown
    line and "3 sent" in the table is reading a report about the *turns*, not about the work.
