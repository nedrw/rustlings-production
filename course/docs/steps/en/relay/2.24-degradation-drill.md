# Runbook v2 and the drills that make it evidence

## RECALL

`ops/runbook-drill@apply` — `mailer/1.22-runbook`: one failure, replayed end to end. The mail server
goes away, the service keeps accepting signups, the retry budget runs out, and the drill reports
whether anything was lost — once with the server coming back and once with it never coming back. The
second run is what makes the first one evidence, and the page (Symptoms, Signals, Steps,
Verification, Escalation) is written from the drill so the two cannot drift apart.

## DELTA

That drill was a **single failure in a service with one background job**: the mail server is away,
the queue is in the database, and the answer to the incident is one sentence — "the queue holds it,
and here is what gets given up on". This track's service has more ways to be in trouble than it has
places to look, and the incident that matters is the one nobody diagnosed:

- **the failure is a mode, not a thing.** "The database is away" is not the same incident as "the
  subscriber stopped answering", and neither is "one tenant is flooding the queue". Each has its own
  signature (`relay/2.18-delivery-metrics`), its own blast radius (one worker's leases, one
  subscriber's deliveries, one tenant's backlog) and — this is the point — its own *correct* answer.
  A responder who reaches for the answer that fits the wrong mode makes the incident longer: raising
  the seats fixes a slow subscriber and makes a storm worse;
- **the drill has to cover the ways the *service* degrades**, not the ways one dependency does. The
  claim each drill makes is the same and it is the service's own: every delivery that was accepted
  ends in `sent` or `dead`, and nothing is left in `pending`, `claimed` or `retrying`. Three
  scenarios, one promise, and each drill is a command somebody types twice;
- **the deliverable is a matrix.** `mailer/1.22` had one replay and one page. This is
  `Scenario::{DatabaseAway, SlowSubscriber, TenantStorm} × Recovery::{ComesBack, Never}` — six
  worlds, of which three are the promise and three are the falsification — plus a page that names
  the command for each of the three, because a runbook whose drills cannot be run per incident is a
  runbook nobody opens during one.

Where this sits between its neighbours: `ops/rolling-upgrade@apply` (`relay/2.22`) is the drill for
*changing* the service safely, and `db/schema-online-evolution@aware` (`relay/2.23`) is the schema
half of it; this step is what happens when nothing is being deployed and something is still wrong.
And `delivery/dlq@apply` (`relay/2.10`) is not a degradation: a delivery in the DLQ was given up on
*deliberately*, and `Report::nothing_was_lost` counts it as an ending rather than as a loss — the
drills are about the deliveries that are still owed, which is the state nobody notices.

## TASK

The scaffold put two files in place, and each has a hole the other cannot fill:
`tests/support/drill.rs` shapes three drills and reports a `Report` it *decided* rather than one it
measured; `docs/runbook.md` has the sections an incident needs and placeholder targets.

### `tests/support/drill.rs` — the drills

`Scenario` picks the failure, `Recovery` picks whether the broken thing comes back, and `run` /
`run_with` are already wired: they start a fixture (a database and a service of its own, two
tenants), accept events, and then read the report from the service's records. What is wrong is what
happens in between — the three scenario functions are yours. Each one has the same four jobs:

1. **make the failure real.** `DatabaseAway`: the worker's own pool claims every delivery and is then
   *closed* under it — the rows are stamped with a holder that will never answer. `SlowSubscriber`:
   the fixture's subscriber takes every attempt and parks it (`tests/support/load.rs`), so a full set
   of attempts is in flight and none is answered — and note that one subscriber is one *lane*
   (`relay/2.9-ordering`), so a drill that wants four attempts in flight registers four endpoints.
   `TenantStorm`: one tenant's flood is queued and the other tenant's single event is behind it;
2. **wait for the failure to be observable**, not for a number of seconds. The drill asserts the
   *service's* rows say what the failure did — everything claimed, every lane occupied — before
   anything is decided about the recovery. A recovery that arrives before the failure has happened
   proves nothing about either world;
3. **obey `Recovery`.** `ComesBack` lets the broken thing work again and the drill waits for the
   queue to move (a new worker reclaims what the dead one held; the parked attempts are let through;
   the storm ends and the seats drain it). `Never` leaves it broken, and the drill reports what is
   *still owed* — which for the storm means the flood keeps arriving faster than the seats drain, and
   the drill stops after a bounded number of turns rather than pretending it can win;
4. **read the numbers from the rows** — `sent`, `dead`, and the three states that mean "still owed".
   `Report::nothing_was_lost` is that last count being zero, and it is the whole promise: a delivery
   that was given up on is an ending (it is in the DLQ, and the count is `dead`), while a delivery in
   `claimed` after everything recovered is a delivery nobody will ever attempt.

One thing worth doing deliberately: the two `Panics` in the fixture are features. `Counted` fails a
drill that waits for an attempt that never starts, and the drill's own `assert_eq!`s fail it when the
failure was not real — so a drill that comes apart does so with a sentence about what it was waiting
for, rather than by reporting a green nothing.

### `docs/runbook.md` — the page

The five sections from `mailer/1.22` are already there and they are the right five: **Symptoms** (what
is noticed first, from the outside), **Signals** (what to look at, and *where* — the metric, the
query, the log line), **Steps** (what to do, including the counter-intuitive ones), **Verification**
(what "recovered" means, as numbers), **Escalation** (who to wake up, and what to hand over). What
this version has to add is the matrix: a responder has to be able to tell the three degradations
apart *from the signals*, and each section has to say something different for each one.

The `links` gate runs over this page and every other markdown file in the project: relative targets
have to exist, `#anchors` have to match a heading, and a `TODO` where a target should be is a
failure. The spec checks what a gate cannot: that the page names the exact command for each of the
three drills, and the signals those commands are read against.

### What the spec judges

| the test | the claim |
| --- | --- |
| `the_database_away_drill_keeps_the_promise_and_can_be_replayed` | the worker's pool dies holding every delivery, the lease brings them back, and the queue drains — twice, on two databases |
| `the_slow_subscriber_drill_keeps_the_promise_and_can_be_replayed` | every attempt is in flight and none is answered; they are let through and every delivery is sent — twice |
| `the_tenant_storm_drill_keeps_the_promise_and_can_be_replayed` | the flood ends, the queue drains, and the quiet tenant — whose one event is *newer* than every row of the flood — was served |
| `the_{database_away,slow_subscriber,tenant_storm}_drill_reports_the_loss_when_nothing_recovers` | the same three drills with the broken thing never coming back each report outstanding work in the *service's* rows: the drills can fail, which is what makes the green runs mean something |
| `the_runbook_names_its_sections_its_drills_and_the_signals_they_are_read_against` | five sections, the three exact drill commands, `relay_backlog_depth` and `relay_deliveries_total{outcome="dead"}`, and no placeholders |

## DONE WHEN

- `kiln run relay/2.24-degradation-drill` — the spec passes (seven cases above), in under a minute
- `kiln check relay/2.24-degradation-drill` — every must gate is green, `links` included
- each of these is a command you have run yourself, and you have read the `Never` report of at least
  one of them:

```sh
cargo test -p relay --test spec_degradation_drill the_database_away_drill
cargo test -p relay --test spec_degradation_drill the_slow_subscriber_drill
cargo test -p relay --test spec_degradation_drill the_tenant_storm_drill
```

- the page tells a responder, in order: what they are looking at, where to look, what to do, how to
  know it worked, and who to wake up when it does not — **and** how to tell the three degradations
  apart before doing any of it
- you can say out loud why a delivery in `dead` is not a lost delivery and a delivery in `claimed`
  is, and which of the two the drill is about

## TRAP

1. **A drill that always passes.** `Report` that says `delivered == accepted, outstanding == 0` for
   every world — which is exactly what the scaffold starts as — is the most common shape of a written
   drill and it is worse than none: it turns an incident into "the drill is green, so it must be
   something else". The `Never` cases exist to fail it; run them.
2. **A recovery that beats the failure.** Releasing the parked attempts before they are parked, or
   letting the database come back before the claims are held, produces two worlds that look the same
   and a drill that proves nothing about either. Wait for the *rows* to say the failure happened.
3. **Reading the report from the drill's intentions.** `delivered = accepted` is a number the drill
   knows without asking anybody. The service's own records are the only place the answer lives — see
   `Report::nothing_was_lost` for the three states that count as "still owed".
4. **A sleep instead of a wait.** `thread::sleep(2s)` is the second most common shape: the fastest
   green run on an idle machine and a flake on a loaded one. Every wait in these drills is a
   condition with a deadline (`Counted` fails with its counters in the message), and the *lease* case
   is handled by aging the lease rather than by waiting one out.
5. **One subscriber for four attempts.** `relay/2.9-ordering` gives each endpoint a lane, so attempts
   to one subscriber are serialised *on purpose*: a slow-subscriber drill with one endpoint sees one
   attempt in flight and "nothing lost" for a reason that has nothing to do with the failure.
6. **Believing "nothing was lost" means "everybody got everything".** A delivery that spent its retry
   budget is `dead`: the service gave up on it, it is in the DLQ, and pretending otherwise makes the
   drill about the subscriber rather than about this service's promise. The promise is that nothing
   is *invisible* — and `dead` is the opposite of invisible.
7. **A runbook written from the implementation.** A page written by reading `src/` lists settings and
   code paths, which is exactly what the responder cannot use at three in the morning. Write it from
   the alert that would page you, then check that the alert exists — and if it does not, that is a
   finding about the service, not about the page.
8. **A page whose commands are one command.** "Run the drills" is not a verification step per
   incident. The three commands are different because the three failures are, and the spec checks
   that each one is on the page.
9. **Escalation as an afterthought.** "Contact the team" is not escalation. What the next person needs
   is what you ruled out, what you saw, and the decision you are handing them — including the two
   nobody likes: stop taking events for a tenant, and tell a customer that their events are being
   given up on.
