# Per-tenant rate limits that cannot starve each other

## WHY

Ingesting an event is the cheapest thing this service does and the most attractive thing to flood:
one request writes one row, and every row is then owed to every endpoint the tenant registered. A
tenant with a loop in their producer — a retry without a backoff, a queue replay, a bug that fires
on every page view — sends a hundred thousand events in a minute, and the cost does not stop at
their own account:

- **the workers are shared.** Every accepted event becomes a delivery to attempt, and the queue is
  one queue: the next tenant's invoices arrive after the flood has been worked through;
- **the database is shared.** Every event is a row and a fan-out inside a transaction, and the
  connection pool is eight connections for everybody;
- **and the failure is not the flood's.** The tenant who broke is fine — they are being *accepted*.
  The tenants who are not being served are the ones whose deliveries are late, and the first
  question anybody asks is "is this service slow", which is exactly the question a rate limit turns
  into a specific answer.

So the ingest path needs a limit. The interesting part is *whose* limit it is:

- **one bucket shared by everyone** is a shared fate. The tenant with the loop spends the quota of
  every other tenant, and the service's answer to a customer asking why they are being refused is
  "somebody else was busy" — which is both true and unacceptable;
- **no bucket for the anonymous** is a hole. Every request that carries no credential still costs a
  database lookup before it is refused, and that lookup is the cheap thing an attacker can ask for
  a hundred thousand times a minute;
- **a refusal with no "when"** turns a limit into a retry storm. A client that is told only "429"
  comes back at once, and now the limit is the load.

The shape is a **token bucket per caller**: `burst` requests available immediately, refilled at
`per_second`, and a `429` with a `Retry-After` when the bucket is empty. The caller is identified by
the credential they presented — hashed, so the limiter needs no lookup to decide — and every request
that presented nothing shares one bucket, because a source address is a claim the network makes
rather than an identity.

**And the boundary that has to be said out loud**: this is *admission* control, and it is aimed at
the caller. `delivery/retry-budget@design` — step `relay/2.8-retry-budget` — has a retry budget and a
breaker, and they are aimed at the **downstream**: the budget decides what this service does about a
subscriber that is failing. The two are not the same thing and they do not substitute for each
other: a bucket never sends anything (it only refuses), and a breaker never refuses anything (it
only stops trying). A caller's quota does not protect a subscriber from a slow queue, and a breaker
does not protect this service from a caller that sends ten thousand events a second. `relay/2.14`
is the third side of that triangle — how much work this process holds at once, whoever sent it.

## TASK

The scaffold added `src/ratelimit.rs`: `Quota`, `Clock`, `Bucket`, `Verdict`, `Limiter` and
`bucket_of`, with `todo!()` bodies. It imports no crate: the arithmetic here is about thirty lines,
it has to be deterministic under a clock a test owns, and `governor` (or any other limiter crate)
would be a dependency bought to avoid writing a subtraction.

### `src/lib.rs` and `src/ratelimit.rs`

- `Quota::new(burst, per_second)` — a quota, or `Error::Config` when either number is zero. A bucket
  of nothing, or one that never refills, refuses every request for ever: that is a deployment taking
  its own service down with a typo, and it is refused where it is read.
- `Bucket` and its two operations: `refill` adds `elapsed × per_second`, capped at `burst` (an hour
  of idleness is one burst, not an hour of requests); `is_full` is the state a bucket nobody has
  spent from is in.
- `Limiter::check(caller) -> Verdict` — refill the caller's bucket, then spend one request or answer
  `Verdict::Refused { retry_after }` with `(1 - left) / per_second`. One lock covers the read, the
  refill and the decrement: two steps would admit more than the bucket holds.
- `Limiter::known()` — how many buckets are held. The map is bounded: once it is larger than
  `SWEEP_AT`, the buckets that are *full again* are dropped, because a full bucket and no bucket at
  all behave identically. Without that, a limiter grows with the number of credentials an attacker
  invents.
- `Clock` — the port (`now() -> Duration`, monotonic, only differences matter), with `System` over
  `Instant` for deployments.
- `bucket_of(Option<&str>) -> String` — the bucket: the credential, **hashed** (a key that lives in
  a map for the length of a run is a key in a core dump), or `ANONYMOUS` when there was none.

### `src/config.rs`

Add a `rate_limit` section of type `ratelimit::Quota` (the limiter's own type, so a zero cannot
exist to be misread — `Quota::new` is the only constructor), from `rate_limit.burst` /
`rate_limit.per_second` or `RELAY__RATE_LIMIT__BURST` / `RELAY__RATE_LIMIT__PER_SECOND`, defaults
`32` and `16`. A value that is not a whole number is a configuration error. Report both lines in
`--check-config`.

### `src/error.rs` and `src/http.rs`

- `Error::RateLimited { retry_after }` — status **429**, kind `"rate_limited"`, `retryable: true`.
  The `IntoResponse` for `Error` also writes `Retry-After` (whole seconds, never zero) whenever the
  error carries a wait, so the header travels with the refusal wherever it is raised.
- `AppState` holds one `Limiter` for the process, built from the configuration over `System`.
- One layer, `limit_calls`, on the tenant routes: `bucket_of(bearer_token(headers).as_deref())`,
  then `check`. **It goes *outside* authentication** — the last `.layer()` applied is the outermost
  one — so a request over quota is refused before the lookup that would have identified it, and an
  anonymous flood is refused without touching the database at all.

### `src/openapi.rs`

Every route of a tenant can answer `429` now, so every one of them describes it (`relay/2.3`'s rule:
a status a caller can get has to be in the contract). The header belongs in the description.

## TRAP

1. **One bucket for the whole service.** Correct arithmetic, wrong policy: one tenant's loop refuses
   everybody, and the metric that would have shown it ("refused, per tenant") does not exist. The
   spec's first test is exactly this: acme is refused over quota, and globex's next request is
   `201`.
2. **Deciding the bucket by looking the key up.** Then a request is authenticated before it is
   limited, and a flood of requests with invented keys costs one database round trip each before
   being refused — the load you were trying to shed is the load you are paying for. The credential
   is hashed instead, which needs no lookup at all.
3. **Counting outside the lock.** `if bucket.left >= 1.0 { … }` with the decrement somewhere else,
   or a `Mutex` released between the read and the write, admits more than the bucket holds: eight
   threads can each read "one left". The spec runs exactly that with a frozen clock.
4. **Refilling past the burst.** Adding elapsed × rate without a cap hands a caller who was idle for
   an hour a bucket holding three thousand requests — the burst they waited for, in one instant,
   which is the opposite of what a bucket is for.
5. **Rounding the fractional token away.** `left.floor()` on every check means half a second of
   refill earns nothing and gets *forgotten*: the effective rate becomes `1 / ceil(1/rate)` seconds
   per request, which is slower than the setting, and it depends on how often the caller asks.
6. **A refill driven by a timer.** A task per caller, or one global ticker, is a thing to leak, to
   drift and to shut down — and it makes the limiter's behaviour depend on the runtime's scheduling
   rather than on elapsed time. Refill on read: `bucket.refill(now)` and nothing else.
7. **A bucket map that only grows.** The key is whatever credential the caller presented, and an
   attacker invents one per request: a map with no eviction is a memory leak with an attacker's
   schedule. Full buckets are the ones nobody is spending from.
8. **`Retry-After: 0`.** Truncating the wait to whole seconds gives `0` for anything under a second,
   which tells a client to retry immediately — the storm the header exists to prevent. Never less
   than one second, and always an integer, because that is what the header means.
9. **Refusing with a body nobody can act on.** A bare `429` with an empty body leaves a client with
   nothing to parse and an operator with nothing to grep. The body is the same `Error` shape as
   every other refusal, with `error: "rate_limited"` and `retryable: true`.
10. **Putting the limiter inside authentication and calling it equivalent.** Then the anonymous flood
    is 401'd after a lookup, and the "cheap refusal" is not cheap. Where the layer sits *is* the
    behaviour: outside authentication, on the tenant routes, with the shared anonymous bucket.

  bucket, and what happens when it is empty", including the per-account plus per-endpoint split

  interface, then compare it with `SWEEP_AT` and a `Clock` a test moves
- [Cloudflare: how we built rate limiting](https://blog.cloudflare.com/counting-things-a-lot-of-different-things/)
  — why a token bucket and a sliding window disagree, and where the counting has to happen instead
- [Rust: `Instant`](https://doc.rust-lang.org/std/time/struct.Instant.html) — monotonic by
  definition, which is what makes it the right clock for a bucket and the wrong one for a timestamp
