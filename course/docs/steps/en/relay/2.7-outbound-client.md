# An outbound client you can reason about: deadlines, keep-alive, redirects

## WHY

A delivery is one HTTP request to somebody else's server, and every failure of that request is a
failure of *time*. A subscriber that accepts the connection and answers nothing holds the worker
forever. One that answers in forty seconds holds it for forty seconds — and with a server default of
"no read timeout at all", a handful of such subscribers hold every worker, the queue stops moving,
and nothing in the logs says why. The events pile up behind a target that is not even down.

So the deadlines are the subject of this step, and there are three because they answer three
different questions:

- **connect** — how long the handshake may take. A host that is filtered, routable-only-in-theory,
  or simply gone;
- **read** — how long *one* read may take before bytes arrive. A target that took the connection and
  went quiet;
- **the attempt** — how long everything together may take, redirects included. Without it, a chain of
  four hops that each answer inside their own read deadline has no bound at all: the read deadline is
  per read, and the attempt is the only total.

Two more decisions come with the client, and both are about the connection rather than the payload:
a connection is **reused** (a worker that opens a socket — and pays for a TLS handshake, wherever
that happens — per delivery spends more time connecting than delivering), and a **redirect** is
followed by a policy that counts hops and refuses loops rather than following whatever a target says.

Finally, the client is not the component that decides what to do about a failure: it classifies the
answer into the three words the delivery layer acts on, and the delivery layer decides. A client that
retries on its own is a second retry policy nobody can see.

### `src/outbound.rs`

- `Client::attempt(&self, request)` — one attempt, and the three deadlines applied where they belong:
  the whole thing inside `deadline`, each connection inside `connect`, each read inside `read`. A
  deadline that expires is `Retryable`: nothing was delivered, and nothing about the subscriber was
  learned that says otherwise.
- **The request on the wire**: a request line, `host`, the caller's headers, `content-length`, the
  body. `connection`, `keep-alive`, `te`, `trailer`, `transfer-encoding`, `upgrade` and the
  `proxy-*` set are **hop-by-hop**: they describe a connection, and this client owns the connection
  it is about to use, so they are dropped rather than forwarded. Everything else is passed through
  exactly as written.
- **The answer**: a status line, headers, and a body that ends one of two ways — at the
  `content-length` the answer promised, or at the close of the connection when it promised none.
  Both are in the wild; reading only the first loses every body from a target that streams and
  closes. An answer that stops early is *unfinished*, not short: it is retryable.
- **Keep-alive**: an answer that does not close the connection leaves it reusable, and the client
  keeps it for the same authority (`Client` is shared as `&self`, so the pool lives behind a lock).
  An answer that says `connection: close` ends that connection, and the next attempt opens a new one.
  A `content-length`-less body ends at the close, so it is never reusable.
- **Redirects**: follow a `Location` — absolute or relative — up to `Policy::redirects` hops, refuse
  a URL the attempt has already visited (a loop), refuse a hop that has no `Location`, and refuse a
  hop this client cannot speak (https). A `303` becomes a `GET` with no body; a `301`/`302` in front
  of a webhook means "send the same thing here instead", so the method and the body are kept.
- **Classification** (`Outcome`): `2xx` success; `408` and `429` retryable (the subscriber is asking
  for another attempt); every other `4xx` permanent; `5xx` retryable; anything this client does not
  understand, and every refusal of the redirect policy, permanent. A permanent answer does not get
  another attempt — spending the retry budget on a `410` is the budget the next delivery needs.
- `HttpSender` — the `relay/2.6-at-least-once` port, on top of the client: one attempt is one POST to
  the endpoint's URL, the body is the event as the API answers with it, and `x-relay-delivery`
  carries the delivery id, which is stable across attempts and is what the receiver deduplicates on.

What this client deliberately does **not** do, and each one is a sentence rather than a silence:

- **`https`.** A delivery target may be `https`, and this client refuses it with a reason
  (`Permanent`, because retrying will not grow a TLS implementation). Where TLS belongs — in the
  client with `rustls`, or at a terminator in front of the process — is a deployment decision, and
  the step that decides it is not this one.
- **chunked transfer-encoding.** A subscriber that answers chunked is not understood: the body reads
  as framing characters. This is a *liar's* answer rather than a missing feature, and the fix is a
  decoder in this file, not a longer deadline.
- **interim `1xx` responses** (`Expect: 100-continue`): a `100 Continue` line is read as if it were
  the answer. Nothing this service sends asks for one.
- **userinfo and IPv6 literals in the authority** (`http://user@host/`, `http://[::1]/`): the URL
  parser of step `relay/2.1-project-skeleton` accepts them and this client's "host:port or host"
  split does not. Refusing them is a smaller change than supporting them, and the subscriber URLs
  this service registers are its tenants' own.

## TRAP

1. **One deadline.** `tokio::time::timeout(30s, whole_attempt)` is the version everybody writes, and
   it hides the difference between "the subscriber was quiet for ten seconds" and "the subscriber
   took ten seconds to answer each of four hops". Without a *per-read* deadline, a target that sends
   one byte a second is inside the attempt deadline the whole way; without an attempt deadline, a
   chain has no total.
2. **Reading the body to EOF.** `read_to_end` on an HTTP/1.1 connection with `content-length` never
   returns until the target closes — which a keep-alive target does not do. The worker waits for the
   read deadline on every successful delivery, and the connection cannot be reused because it is
   still being read. `content-length` is the end of the body; the close is the fallback, not the rule.
3. **Trusting a short body.** `content-length: 100` and five bytes on the wire is an answer that
   never finished. Storing those five bytes delivers half a payload and records it as delivered
   — the one failure at-least-once promises not to have, produced by the client rather than the
   queue. The spec's last case is this exact answer.
4. **Forwarding hop-by-hop headers.** Copying the caller's `connection: close`, `transfer-encoding` or
   `te` into the next hop describes a connection that no longer exists: `transfer-encoding: chunked`
   tells the target to read a body framing this client did not write, and `connection: close` turns
   a connection the client means to reuse into one the target will close underneath it.
5. **A new connection per delivery.** Correct, and slow: every delivery pays a handshake, the
   subscriber sees a burst of new connections instead of a stream of events, and under load the
   connect queue (and the file descriptors) are the bottleneck. Reuse per authority, and take
   `connection: close` at its word.
6. **A pooled connection that is dead.** A target that closed an idle connection leaves a socket that
   fails on the first write. It is retryable, not fatal — and it is why an attempt that fails on a
   *reused* connection must not put that connection back in the pool.
7. **Following redirects forever, or not at all.** `Location` is a stranger's instruction: unbounded
   following is a delivery that walks across somebody's load balancer, and a loop is the same thing
   with no end. The other half of the trap is a client that refuses *every* redirect: subscribers
   move, and a service that cannot follow one loses them silently.
8. **Turning a webhook POST into a GET on a `302`.** It is what a browser does, and it means the
   payload never arrives — the target receives an empty request and answers `200`, and the delivery
   is recorded as delivered. `303` means `GET`; `301`/`302` are "the same thing, over there".
9. **Retrying on the client's own initiative.** A client that retries a 5xx internally hides the
   attempt from the retry budget (step `relay/2.8-retry-budget`), from the metrics
   (`relay/2.18-delivery-metrics`), and from the operator reading `attempts` on the row. One attempt
   is one request, and the decision belongs one layer up.
10. **A client that decides what a status means for the *delivery*.** The classification here is
    about the attempt: a `410` is permanent *for this delivery* because the subscriber said so, and a
    `400` is permanent because retrying it will produce the same `400`. What to do about a
    permanently refused delivery is `mark_dead`, which is the worker's call, not the client's.
