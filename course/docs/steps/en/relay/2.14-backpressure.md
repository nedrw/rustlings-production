# Bounded concurrency: the queue has a bottom

## WHY

The events arrive whether or not anybody can be reached. That is the promise this service makes —
accept now, deliver later — and under load it produces a question nobody asks until it is too late:

> how much of that work is in *this* process?

Three different numbers answer it, and a service dies of confusing them:

- **the rows nobody has delivered.** `deliveries` is the queue, and its depth is a number an operator
  can read. It is allowed to grow: the whole design is that it does, because a subscriber that is
  down for an hour should not stop the events after it from being accepted. What it must not do is
  grow *forever* — past some bound, the honest answer to the next event is `503`, *come back*, not
  another row nothing is going to work through;
- **the rows this worker has claimed.** A claim moves rows to `claimed`, and a `claimed` row is
  invisible to every other worker until its lease expires. That is the right mechanism for one
  attempt at a time and the wrong number to be large: a worker that claims five thousand rows and
  then attempts them one after another holds five thousand rows nobody else can touch, in memory, as
  five thousand tasks;
- **the attempts actually in flight.** This is the number that decides whether the process lives or
  dies. Every in-flight attempt is a socket, a buffer, and a task; a thousand of them is a memory
  profile, and it is *invisible* in the queue metrics because the rows look outstanding — which is
  exactly what they would look like if the worker were healthy.

The step is called backpressure, but the mechanism is a bound, and the useful shape of it is: **the
number of rows a worker claims is the number of attempts it can have in flight**. One permit per
attempt, one claim per turn sized to the permits, and the thing that grows under load is the row
count in `deliveries` — which is where it belongs, because the database is designed to hold a queue
and the process is not.

There is a second bound in the same shape, one level up: past `max_backlog` undelivered events, the
ingest path answers `503`. The transfer matters here — `async/backpressure@aware`, step
`mailer/1.14-worker`, taught exactly this with a bounded in-memory channel and a `503` when it was
full, and the sentence that carried it was "capacity is taken before the work it is for". The
mechanism is different here, because the queue is not in memory: it is the `deliveries` table, the
count is asked of the database, and the check happens *inside the transaction that writes the work* —
so a refused event writes nothing at all, and a caller that retries gets a fresh chance rather than a
half-accepted promise.

**And the boundary that has to be said out loud**, because three steps now have a limit in them:

- `delivery/ordering@apply` — `relay/2.9-ordering` — is about *which* attempts may overlap: one at a
  time per endpoint, because a subscriber's events have an order. That lane is taken inside the
  attempt and this step does not touch it. This step is about how many may overlap **in total**;
- `http/rate-limit@apply` — `relay/2.13-rate-limit` — is about how much one *caller* may ask for, and
  it refuses at the door. This is about what the service may *hold*, whoever sent it: a tenant inside
  their quota can still fill the queue if nobody is delivering;
- `delivery/retry-budget@design` — `relay/2.8-retry-budget` — is about how much this service spends on
  one failing subscriber. A bounded worker makes that budget matter: with the bound in place, a
  subscriber that is down leaves rows in the table instead of consuming the process.

There is one more thing this step is about, and it is not a bound at all: **whose** work is taken.
`claim::claim` takes the oldest rows, which is right, and which also means that when one tenant floods,
the oldest rows are always that tenant's. A worker that claims strictly in queue order serves the
flood until the flood is over — with `max_in_flight` seats, a hundred thousand events is hours in
which every other tenant's delivery waits. Fairness is therefore part of the bound: the seats are
dealt out between the tenants that have work, one round at a time.

## TASK

The scaffold added `src/concurrency.rs` (types and signatures with `todo!()` bodies) and
`tests/support/load.rs` — a subscriber that counts how many attempts are in flight and can be *held*,
which is how the spec observes a bound instead of measuring a clock.

### `src/lib.rs` and `src/concurrency.rs`

- `Limits::new(in_flight, backlog)` — the two numbers, or `Error::Config` when either is zero: a
  service with no seats delivers nothing, and a backlog of nothing refuses every event.
- `has_room(executor, limits)` — the count of undelivered work (`pending`, `retrying`, `claimed`)
  against the bound. It takes a **sqlx executor**, not a pool, because it is asked inside the ingest
  transaction; it is a policy and not a lock, so the queue can end up one row over its bound, which
  nothing can observe.
- `Gate` — `Arc<Semaphore>`-backed seats, `limit()`, `in_flight()`, `enter()` (an **owned** permit,
  so the task that attempts a delivery can hold it across the whole attempt), and
  `quiesce(deadline)`: wait until nothing is in flight, or the deadline passes. The wait is on the
  permits themselves — taking every seat at once can only succeed when nobody holds one — rather than
  on a counter that gets polled.
- `Worker::new(pool, sender, gate, lanes, budget, limits, name)` — the value a deployment runs. The
  gate and the lanes are `Arc`s: two workers in one process share one bound and one set of lanes, and
  a gate per worker is a bound per worker.
- `Worker::turn()` — claim at most `limits.in_flight()` rows with `claim_fair`, take a seat *before*
  each attempt starts, hold it until the record is written, and join every task.
- `Worker::work(turns)` — that, `turns` times.
- `claim_fair(pool, worker, limit, lease)` — the seats dealt out between the tenants that have work,
  oldest waiting tenant first, calling `claim::for_tenant` for each. A tenant with less work than its
  share uses what it has; the leftovers are claimed again next turn.

### `src/claim.rs`

`for_tenant(pool, worker, tenant, limit, lease)` — the claim of `relay/2.5-atomic-claim`, narrowed to
one tenant: the same statement, the same lease, one more condition. It lives here, next to the claim
it narrows, because this module owns the rows and the column names. Factor the row mapping (the
`returning` tuple to `Claimed`) so both claims share it.

### `src/config.rs`, `src/events.rs`, `src/openapi.rs`

- `delivery.max_in_flight` (default 4) and `delivery.max_backlog` (default 10000), as
  `delivery.limits: concurrency::Limits` — the limiter's own type, so a zero cannot be stored.
  Report both in `--check-config`.
- `events::ingest` takes the limits and, **inside the transaction that accepts the event**, refuses
  when the work it would be holding is past the bound: roll back and answer `Error::Unavailable`. A
  replay never reaches that branch — a caller retrying a request it is unsure about must be told
  "you already sent this", not "come back" — which is a property of *where* the check goes.
- The document gains `503` for `POST /events` (the answer a caller can now get), and nothing else
  changes: `429` is the caller's limit, `503` is the service's.

## TRAP

1. **Claiming a batch bigger than the seats.** `claim(pool, worker, 1000, lease)` followed by a loop
   that attempts them is the shape everybody writes first: it works, the queue drains, and the
   process holds a thousand rows and a thousand tasks. Under one slow subscriber it is a bigger
   process each turn — and, because the rows are `claimed`, work no other worker can take either.
   The claim is the bound, or the bound is a lie.
2. **A gate per worker.** `Gate::new(limits)` inside the worker's loop gives every worker its own
   `in_flight` — four workers, four times the bound, and no single place where the process's own
   limit is written down. The gate is an `Arc` the workers share, and it is created once per process.
3. **A borrowed permit in a spawned task.** `SemaphorePermit<'_>` cannot outlive the turn that
   spawned the task, so the attempt cannot be moved into `tokio::spawn` at all — the fix is an
   *owned* permit (`acquire_owned`), and the reason is the reason the seat exists: it is held for the
   length of an HTTP request, which is a lifetime the turn's stack does not have.
4. **`quiesce` by polling a counter.** `while gate.in_flight() > 0 { sleep(10ms) }` is the version
   that works and is always slightly wrong: it sleeps a fixed interval after the last attempt has
   already finished, and it turns "is anything in flight" into a question about the machine's
   scheduler. Taking every seat at once *is* the question, and the runtime answers it.
5. **A claim that divides by the number of tenants.** With no tenant waiting, `limit / 0` is a panic
   in the worker loop — in a library, in production, on the happy path (the queue was empty). Both
   guards (`limit <= 0`, and nobody waiting) are ordinary answers rather than errors.
6. **Dealing the whole batch to one tenant.** `claim_fair` that ignores the shares is `claim::claim`
   with more code and the same starvation: the flood's next thousand rows are older than everybody
   else's. The seats are per tenant, and the leftovers go to the tenants that have been waiting
   longest.
7. **Choosing the address of the check.** `has_room` asked *before* the insert says "there was room a
   moment ago"; asked *after* the commit it cannot refuse anything. Inside the transaction it is the
   only version where a refusal writes nothing, which is what makes `503` safe for a client to retry.
8. **Refusing a replay.** A caller that retried a request it never got an answer to has to be told
   "you already sent this" — the same key, the same event, `200`. If the bound is checked before the
   idempotency lookup, that caller gets a `503` for an event this service already accepted, and the
   client retries a third time.
9. **Counting the backlog on every ingest and calling it free.** The query is a scan of the queue
   table: at ten thousand rows it is nothing, at ten million it is the ingest path's cost. A
   deployment at that size keeps a counter (a trigger, or a gauge the worker maintains) instead of
   asking the truth; this course keeps the query that cannot drift, and says so.
10. **Believing the bound is a lock.** Two ingests can both find room and both write, so the queue can
    be one row over its bound — the same trade `mailer/1.14` made between an exact bound and a queue
    in front of the queue. A bound that has to be exact needs a lock per ingest, and that lock is
    itself the bottleneck.
